## What AI Actually Is

Forget the sci-fi version. Forget sentient robots. Forget Skynet.

Here's what AI actually is right now: **a very good text prediction engine.**

Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4, Claude, and Gemini work by predicting the next word in a sequence. That's it. You give it some text, and it figures out what word should come next. Then it predicts the next one after that. And the next one. Over and over until it has a full response.

This sounds underwhelming until you realize what happens when you train that prediction engine on most of the text humans have ever written. It absorbs patterns from code, medical papers, legal contracts, recipes, poetry, business plans, customer support scripts, and everything in between.

The result is a system that can write like a marketer, think through problems like an analyst, explain things like a teacher, and generate code like a developer. Not because it "understands" any of those things the way you do. But because it has absorbed enough patterns to produce incredibly useful output.

That's the gap between what AI is and what AI does. What it *is* - a next-word predictor. What it *does* - something that looks a lot like thinking.

You don't need to understand the math. You need to understand the implication: if you can describe what you want clearly enough, AI can probably produce a decent first draft.

## The 3 Types of AI Tools

Every AI tool you'll encounter fits into one of three buckets.

### 1. Chatbots

These are conversational AI tools. You type, they respond. You have a back-and-forth conversation.

- **ChatGPT** (OpenAI) - the one that started the current wave. Good at general tasks, coding, analysis.
- **Claude** (Anthropic) - excellent at long documents, nuanced writing, and following detailed instructions.
- **Gemini** (Google) - strong integration with Google's ecosystem. Good at research tasks.

You'll use these the most. They're your daily driver.

### 2. Generators

These create specific media from your description.

- **Image generators** - Midjourney, DALL-E, Stable Diffusion. Describe what you want, get an image.
- **Video generators** - Runway, Sora, Kling. Still early but improving fast.
- **Music generators** - Suno, Udio. Describe a genre and mood, get a song.

Generators are great for creative work, prototyping visuals, and making content when you don't have a designer on speed dial.

### 3. Agents

These are AI tools that take actions on your behalf. They don't just answer questions - they do things.

- An agent can read your email, draft replies, and schedule meetings.
- An agent can monitor a website and alert you when something changes.
- An agent can run a multi-step research workflow and deliver a report.

Agents are the newest category and the most powerful. We'll go deeper on them in Day 3.

## Hands-On - Your First AI Session

Time to stop reading and start doing. Open [Claude](https://claude.ai) or [ChatGPT](https://chat.openai.com) and try these three tasks.

### Task 1 - Write a LinkedIn Post

Paste this prompt:

```
You are a LinkedIn content strategist who writes posts that get engagement
without being cringe. Write a post about how I just started learning about
AI tools and I'm surprised by how practical they are. Tone: honest, slightly
self-deprecating, no hustle-bro energy. Keep it under 150 words. End with
a question to drive comments.
```

Look at what comes back. It's not perfect. But it's a solid first draft that took you 10 seconds instead of 30 minutes of staring at a blank screen.

### Task 2 - Write a Cold Email

```
Write a cold outreach email to a marketing agency owner. I run a small
web development shop and I want to offer white-label AI chatbot services
for their clients. Keep it under 5 sentences. Sound like a real person,
not a sales robot. Include a specific value proposition.
```

### Task 3 - Write 3 Tweets

```
Write 3 tweets about the difference between AI hype and AI reality.
Tone: smart, slightly contrarian, no emojis. Each tweet should be
a standalone thought. Make them quotable.
```

Notice how each prompt tells the AI **who to be**, **what to produce**, **what tone to use**, and **what constraints to follow**. That's the formula.

## Prompt Engineering Basics

Prompt engineering sounds fancy. It's not. It's the skill of telling AI what you want clearly enough that it gives you something useful.

Here are the four moves that matter:

### Be Specific

Bad: "Write me an email."
Good: "Write a 3-sentence follow-up email to a client who hasn't responded in 2 weeks. Tone: friendly but direct."

Vague input gives you generic output. Specific input gives you useful output.

### Give Context

Bad: "Summarize this article."
Good: "Summarize this article for a product manager who needs to decide whether to invest in this technology. Focus on business impact and risks."

Context tells the AI who the audience is and what matters to them. That changes everything about the output.

### Show Examples

Bad: "Write headlines for my newsletter."
Good: "Write 5 headlines for my newsletter. Here are 3 past headlines that performed well: 'Why I Stopped Using Notion', 'The Tool Nobody Talks About', 'I Spent $500 on AI Tools So You Don't Have To'. Match this style."

Examples are the fastest way to calibrate the AI's output to what you actually want.

### Tell It What Role to Play

Bad: "Help me with my resume."
Good: "You are a tech recruiter at a Fortune 500 company. Review my resume and tell me what would make you put it in the 'yes' pile. Be brutally honest."

Roles activate different "patterns" in the model. A resume reviewed by a "recruiter" reads differently than one reviewed by a "career coach."

## The AI Catalogue - What Problems AI Solves

Here's the practical map. Bookmark this. Come back to it when you're looking for ideas.

### For Individuals

- **Writing** - emails, blog posts, social media, cover letters, thank you notes
- **Learning** - explain any concept at any level, create study guides, quiz yourself
- **Health tracking** - analyze symptoms (not a replacement for a doctor), meal planning, supplement research
- **Travel planning** - build itineraries, find hidden gems, compare options, translate on the go
- **Cooking** - recipes from what's in your fridge, meal prep plans, dietary adjustments
- **Fitness programming** - workout plans, form corrections from video descriptions, periodization
- **Language learning** - conversation practice, grammar correction, vocabulary building
- **Personal finance** - budget analysis, explain investment concepts, compare insurance plans

### For Small Businesses

- **Customer support chatbots** - answer FAQs 24/7, route complex questions to humans
- **Bookkeeping summaries** - turn transaction data into readable reports
- **Content calendars** - plan a month of social media in an hour
- **Appointment scheduling** - AI assistants that handle back-and-forth booking
- **Social media management** - draft posts, suggest hashtags, analyze engagement patterns

### For Companies

- **Data analysis and reporting** - turn raw data into executive summaries
- **Process automation** - identify repetitive workflows and build AI-assisted pipelines
- **Document summarization** - condense 100-page reports into 2-page briefs
- **Hiring screening** - parse resumes, generate interview questions, summarize candidate feedback
- **Competitive intelligence** - monitor competitor moves, summarize industry reports
- **Internal knowledge search** - find answers across scattered docs, wikis, and Slack threads

### For Organizations and Governments

- **Citizen services** - chatbots for permit applications, tax questions, service requests
- **Policy analysis** - summarize legislation, model impact scenarios
- **Fraud detection** - flag anomalies in financial transactions and claims
- **Translation services** - make public services accessible across languages

## What AI Is Bad At

AI is not magic. Here's where it falls apart:

- **Math without tools** - LLMs are text predictors, not calculators. They'll confidently give you wrong math. Always verify numbers or use AI tools with built-in code execution.
- **Real-time data** - most models have a knowledge cutoff. They don't know what happened yesterday unless they have internet access.
- **Nuanced judgment** - AI can present options. It can't make judgment calls that require understanding your specific situation, values, and risk tolerance.
- **Anything requiring physical presence** - AI can plan your home renovation. It can't hold a hammer.
- **Keeping secrets** - don't put confidential data into AI tools unless you understand the privacy policy. Most free tiers use your data for training.
- **Being consistent across long conversations** - after enough back-and-forth, models start contradicting themselves. Keep important conversations focused and relatively short.

Knowing the limits is just as important as knowing the capabilities. The people who get burned by AI are the ones who trust it blindly. The people who get value from it are the ones who treat it like a smart intern - useful, fast, but needs supervision.
