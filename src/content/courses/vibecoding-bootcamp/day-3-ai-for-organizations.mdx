## The 7 Categories of AI Applications

Every AI application in the wild fits into one of seven categories. This isn't a theoretical framework. It's a map of where companies are actually spending money and getting results right now.

Understanding these categories does two things for you. First, it helps you spot opportunities. Second, it gives you vocabulary to pitch AI projects to people who control budgets.

Let's walk through each one.

---

### 1. Content and Communication

**What it is:** Using AI to produce, edit, translate, and distribute written and visual content at scale.

This is the most visible category because it's the one everyone tries first. But the real value isn't "AI writes a blog post." The real value is systematic content operations.

**Real examples:**

- **Jasper** built a $1.5B company helping marketing teams produce brand-consistent content across dozens of channels. Not one blog post - hundreds of pieces per month with consistent voice.
- **Translated** uses AI for professional translation at scale. They train custom models on client-specific terminology so a legal firm's translations read like a lawyer wrote them.
- **Tome** generates presentation decks from a brief. Give it your talking points, get a slide deck with visuals and structure.

**The pattern:** AI doesn't replace writers. It replaces the blank page. First drafts happen in seconds. Humans edit, refine, and add judgment.

---

### 2. Customer Experience

**What it is:** AI that interacts with your customers directly - answering questions, routing issues, personalizing recommendations.

This is where the money is for most businesses. A good AI customer experience tool pays for itself in the first month by deflecting support tickets.

**Real examples:**

- **Intercom's Fin** is an AI agent that resolves up to 50% of support conversations without a human. It reads your help docs and answers questions in natural language. When it can't help, it escalates with full context.
- **Klarna** replaced 700 customer service agents with an AI system that handles 2.3 million conversations per month. Average resolution time dropped from 11 minutes to 2 minutes.
- **Stitch Fix** uses AI to personalize clothing recommendations based on your style profile, purchase history, and feedback. The AI picks items, a human stylist reviews them.

**The pattern:** Start with FAQ deflection. That's the easiest win. Then move to personalized recommendations. Then move to proactive outreach.

---

### 3. Data and Analytics

**What it is:** Turning messy, scattered data into clear insights and predictions.

Most companies are drowning in data and starving for insight. AI bridges that gap.

**Real examples:**

- **Obviously AI** lets non-technical people build prediction models by uploading a spreadsheet. "Will this customer churn?" "What will sales be next quarter?" Point and click.
- **Hex** is a data notebook where you can ask questions about your data in plain English. "What were our top 10 products by revenue last quarter?" It writes the SQL, runs it, and charts the result.
- **Anodot** monitors business metrics and detects anomalies automatically. If your payment failure rate spikes at 3am, Anodot catches it before your customers notice.

**The pattern:** The best data AI tools don't require data science degrees. They let business people ask business questions and get business answers.

---

### 4. Operations and Workflows

**What it is:** AI that automates the repetitive, manual processes that eat up human time.

This is the "boring but lucrative" category. Nobody tweets about operations optimization. But it's where AI delivers the most measurable ROI.

**Real examples:**

- **Coupa** uses AI for procurement optimization. It predicts supply chain disruptions, suggests alternative suppliers, and automates purchase order routing.
- **Tractian** puts AI-powered sensors on factory equipment to predict when machines will fail. Fix it before it breaks, not after the production line stops.
- **Lalamove** uses AI to optimize delivery routes in real time across Southeast Asian cities. More deliveries per driver, less fuel, happier customers.

**The pattern:** Find the process that someone does manually every week. The one with the spreadsheet they hate. That's your AI opportunity.

---

### 5. Knowledge Management

**What it is:** Making an organization's collective knowledge searchable, accessible, and useful.

This is the Second Brain concept from Day 2, scaled to hundreds or thousands of people. And it might be the most underrated category on this list.

**Real examples:**

- **Glean** is an enterprise AI search engine. It connects to your company's Slack, Google Drive, Confluence, Notion, and email. Ask it a question in plain English and it finds the answer across all your tools.
- **Guru** maintains a knowledge base that AI keeps up to date. When information changes, it flags outdated cards and suggests updates.
- **Notion AI** lets teams search across their entire Notion workspace with natural language. "What was the decision about the pricing change last March?" It finds the relevant meeting notes, docs, and comments.

**The pattern:** The value here isn't the search itself. It's the hours saved. McKinsey estimates that employees spend 1.8 hours per day searching for information. Cut that in half and you've added almost a full day of productivity per week per employee.

---

### 6. Creative and Design

**What it is:** AI that generates visual and creative assets from descriptions.

This category is evolving the fastest. What was impossible 18 months ago is now table stakes.

**Real examples:**

- **Canva's Magic Studio** generates designs from text descriptions. "Create a social media post about our summer sale with a beach theme." Done in seconds.
- **Runway** produces video from text prompts and reference images. Marketing teams use it for ad concepts and storyboards.
- **Adobe Firefly** generates and modifies images inside the Creative Cloud tools that designers already use. It fits into existing workflows instead of replacing them.

**The pattern:** AI creative tools are best at first drafts and variations. "Give me 20 versions of this ad with different backgrounds." A designer picks the best one and refines it. Speed goes up 10x, quality stays high.

---

### 7. Decision Support

**What it is:** AI that helps humans make better decisions by processing more information than any person could.

This is the most sophisticated category. AI doesn't make the decision. It presents the analysis so a human can make a better decision faster.

**Real examples:**

- **Palantir AIP** lets military and government analysts ask questions of massive datasets in natural language. "Show me all supply chain disruptions in the last 90 days that affected more than 3 regions."
- **AlphaSense** scans earnings calls, SEC filings, and news to give investment analysts competitive intelligence. "What did every CEO in the semiconductor industry say about AI spending last quarter?"
- **Crayon** monitors competitors automatically. Price changes, new features, hiring patterns, messaging changes. It surfaces the signals that matter.

**The pattern:** Decision support AI works best when the decision is high-stakes and data-rich. The more data involved, the more value AI adds by compressing it into something a human can act on.

## The Boring Niche Framework

Here's a truth that most people in tech ignore: **the best AI startups solve boring problems.**

They don't build flashy demos. They don't go viral on Twitter. They quietly solve unglamorous problems for people who will pay real money for a solution.

Consider these:

- **Invoice processing.** Companies receive thousands of invoices in different formats. Someone has to read each one, extract the amounts, match them to purchase orders, and enter them into the accounting system. AI does this in seconds.
- **Insurance claim routing.** When someone files a claim, it needs to go to the right department, get the right priority level, and trigger the right workflow. AI reads the claim, categorizes it, and routes it.
- **Tenant maintenance requests.** "My faucet is leaking." An AI reads this, determines it's a plumbing issue, checks the vendor schedule, and books the repair. The property manager doesn't touch it.

None of these will win a hackathon. All of them are real businesses making real revenue.

### How to Find Boring Niches

Here's the playbook:

**Watch what people complain about on Reddit.** Go to industry-specific subreddits. r/accounting, r/PropertyManagement, r/smallbusiness, r/legaladvice. Read the complaints. The complaint that shows up every week is your opportunity.

**Find the spreadsheet someone hates updating.** Every industry has one. The tracking sheet that someone spends 4 hours on every Friday. The report that requires pulling data from 6 different systems. That's your AI app.

**Look for the manual process that takes 4 hours every week.** If a person does the same steps repeatedly, and those steps involve reading text, making simple decisions, and entering data somewhere, AI can probably handle 80% of it.

**Talk to people outside of tech.** Developers and designers live in a bubble where everything is already digitized. Talk to the person running a plumbing company, a dental office, a construction firm. Their problems are simpler, less competitive, and they'll pay for solutions.

## The Progression of AI Sophistication

There's a clear ladder here. Each day of this bootcamp corresponds to a level.

### Level 0 - AI as a Writing Tool

This is Day 1. You use ChatGPT or Claude to write emails, posts, and documents. It's useful. It saves time. But you're doing all the thinking. The AI just executes.

Most people stop here. They treat AI as a fancy autocomplete. That's fine, but you're leaving 90% of the value on the table.

### Level 1 - AI-Powered Knowledge Management

This is Day 2. You build a Second Brain with Obsidian and use AI to connect, summarize, and synthesize your notes. Now AI isn't just writing for you. It's helping you think.

This is where you start to feel the compounding effect. The more notes you capture, the smarter your AI-powered system gets at finding patterns.

### Level 2 - AI for Specific Business Workflows

This is today. You identify a specific problem in a specific industry and apply AI to solve it. Invoice processing. Customer support. Content generation. Data analysis.

This is where the money is. Level 2 is where you go from "AI is cool" to "AI is a business."

### Level 3 - AI Agents That Take Autonomous Actions

This is where things get really interesting. And it's where projects like Hermes come in.

## Why Agents Exist

There's a fundamental difference between a chatbot and an agent.

A chatbot waits for you to ask. You type a question. It answers. You type another question. It answers again. The chatbot is reactive. It does nothing until you poke it.

An agent is proactive. You give it goals, tools, and permission to act. Then it goes and does things.

Imagine an AI that:
- Monitors your inbox every morning
- Drafts replies to routine emails
- Flags urgent messages that need your personal attention
- Schedules meetings by coordinating with other people's calendars
- Summarizes your day at 6pm with key decisions and action items

That's not a chatbot. That's an agent. It doesn't wait. It acts.

This is the Hermes-style thinking: AI that operates on your behalf, following rules and goals you've defined, handling the routine so you can focus on the important.

## The Agent Stack

Every AI agent, from a simple email responder to a complex business workflow, is built from the same four components:

### The LLM Brain

This is the thinking engine. GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, or any other language model. It reads inputs, reasons about what to do, and generates outputs. It's the agent's decision-maker.

### Tools

The brain is useless without hands. Tools are the APIs and integrations that let the agent interact with the real world:

- **Email** - read, draft, send
- **Calendar** - check availability, create events
- **Databases** - query records, update entries
- **Web search** - find current information
- **File systems** - read and write documents
- **Messaging** - send Slack messages, notifications

The more tools an agent has, the more it can do. But more tools also means more potential for things to go wrong. Start small.

### Memory

Without memory, every conversation starts from zero. An agent needs to remember:

- **Short-term memory** - what happened earlier in this conversation
- **Long-term memory** - what happened in previous conversations, user preferences, past decisions
- **Working memory** - the current task, its status, and intermediate results

Memory is what separates a toy demo from a useful agent. The agent that remembers you prefer morning meetings and don't like Mondays is dramatically more useful than one that asks every time.

### Goals

This is the most important and most overlooked part. What is the agent optimizing for?

- "Minimize the number of emails I need to read personally"
- "Schedule meetings with minimum calendar fragmentation"
- "Flag any customer support ticket that mentions churn risk"

Clear goals give the agent direction. Vague goals give you an agent that does random stuff. Goals are the difference between a helpful assistant and an annoying one.

## Today's Exercise

Time to put this into practice. You're going to identify AI opportunities.

### Step 1 - Pick 3 Problems

Look at the 7 categories above. Think about your own work, your industry, or the businesses around you. Pick 3 specific problems that AI could solve.

Not "AI for healthcare." That's too vague. Something like "AI that reads radiology reports and flags the ones that need urgent review" or "AI that turns customer support call recordings into searchable knowledge base articles."

### Step 2 - Write a 2-Sentence Pitch

For each problem, write exactly two sentences:
1. What the problem is and who has it.
2. What your AI app does about it.

Example:

> "Property managers waste 3-5 hours per week manually routing maintenance requests to the right vendor. Our AI reads incoming requests, categorizes the issue, checks vendor availability, and books the appointment automatically."

### Step 3 - Score Each Idea

For each of your 3 ideas, rate:

| Criteria | Scale |
|---|---|
| Market size | S / M / L |
| Personal interest | 1-5 |
| Technical feasibility with current AI | 1-5 |
| Competition | Low / Medium / High |

The sweet spot: Medium or Large market, personal interest of 4+, feasibility of 3+, and Low or Medium competition.

### Step 4 - Capture Everything in Your Second Brain

Create a note for each idea in your Obsidian vault under `Projects/` or `Resources/`. Link them to your Day 1 and Day 2 notes. Use Claude to pressure-test your ideas:

```
Here's my idea for an AI application: [paste your 2-sentence pitch].

Poke holes in this. What could go wrong? Who are the likely
competitors? What's the hardest technical challenge? What would
the first version look like?
```

Save the AI's response in your note. This is your Second Brain in action - capturing not just your ideas, but the thinking around them.

Tomorrow we start building. Your ideas become real apps. But first you need the right foundation: the right problems, clearly articulated, with an honest assessment of what's hard and what's feasible. That's what today is about.
