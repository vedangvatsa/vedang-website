<SectionLabel label="Pricing for AI Apps" />

You have users. Some of them like your app. Now you need to figure out how to make money from it.

Pricing is the thing most builders overthink and underprice. Let's fix that.

### The 4 Models

**1. Subscription (the Netflix model)**

Predictable revenue. Works for tools people use daily or weekly. You charge a flat monthly fee regardless of how much they use it.

Example: $19/month for unlimited generations.

Best when: your API costs per user are low and predictable, and the tool is part of someone's daily workflow.

**2. Usage-based (pay per generation)**

Good when your API costs are high or unpredictable. The user pays based on how much they use. You never lose money on a heavy user.

Example: $0.10 per document processed. $0.05 per image generated.

Best when: usage varies wildly between users, and some users would cost you money on a flat subscription.

**3. Freemium (free tier plus paid upgrade)**

Best for growth. Hardest to convert. You let people use a limited version for free and charge for the full experience.

Example: 10 free uses per month, $9/month for unlimited.

Best when: you want viral growth and your free tier is good enough to hook people but limited enough to frustrate power users.

**4. One-time purchase (lifetime access)**

Good for simple tools that don't have ongoing costs. Chrome extensions, utilities, templates.

Example: $49 lifetime for a Chrome extension.

Best when: the tool doesn't call expensive APIs and you don't need recurring revenue.

### Price Anchoring

Always show 3 tiers. Make the middle one "Most Popular." This isn't manipulation - it's decision architecture. People struggle with two choices. Three choices with a recommendation makes the decision easy.

| Tier | Price | What's Included |
|---|---|---|
| Starter | Free | 10 uses/month |
| Pro (Most Popular) | $19/month | Unlimited uses, priority support |
| Team | $49/month | Everything in Pro + 5 team members |

The free tier gets people in the door. The Pro tier is where you make money. The Team tier makes Pro look like a bargain.

### Ramen Profitable Math

Paul Graham coined this term. It means: what's the minimum monthly revenue to cover your costs so you can keep building?

If you spend $2,000/month on living expenses and charge $20/month per user, you need 100 paying users. That's your first real milestone. Not 10,000 users. Not a million in ARR. One hundred people paying you $20.

Write that number down. It's probably smaller than you think.

### The Cost Trap

AI API costs can eat your margins alive if you're not paying attention.

Do this math before setting your price:

1. What does each user cost you in API calls per month?
2. What are you charging per month?
3. What's the margin?

If each user costs you $2/month in API calls and you charge $19/month, your margin is $17. That's healthy.

If each user costs you $8/month and you charge $10/month, your margin is $2. That's trouble. One spike in usage and you're losing money.

If each user costs you $15/month, you need to either charge more, add usage caps, use cheaper models for simple tasks, or cache common responses.

<Callout type="warning" title="Always Cap Usage">
Even on "unlimited" plans, set reasonable usage limits. "Unlimited" means "more than anyone would reasonably need," not "actually infinite." 500 generations per month is "unlimited" for 99% of users. The 1% who would do 50,000 are the ones who'll bankrupt you.
</Callout>

<SectionLabel label="Close Your First Paying Customer" />

Your first paying customer is the hardest one. Every one after that gets easier.

### Warm Outreach First

Start with people who already know your app exists:
- People who signed up during testing
- People who gave positive feedback
- Friends and colleagues who fit the target profile

Send them a direct message. Not a mass email. A personal message.

### The 3-Line Cold Email

For people who don't know you yet:

> I noticed [SPECIFIC THING ABOUT THEM - a post they wrote, a problem they mentioned, their job title].
>
> I built [PRODUCT] that [SOLVES THEIR SPECIFIC PROBLEM - not a generic pitch, the actual problem].
>
> Want to try it free for a week?

That's it. Three lines. No attachments. No pitch deck. No "I'd love to get 15 minutes on your calendar." Just a clear offer.

### Pitching vs. Selling

Pitching is talking about yourself. Your features, your technology, your vision.

Selling is solving their problem. Their workflow, their pain, their wasted time.

Nobody cares about your tech stack. They care about the 3 hours per week they'll get back.

### Handling Objections

**"Too expensive."** Show the ROI. "You currently spend 3 hours per week on this. At your hourly rate, that's $450/month. This tool is $19/month."

**"I already use something else."** Show the switching cost is low. "You can import your existing data in 2 minutes. Try it for a week alongside what you have. If it's not better, cancel."

**"I need to think about it."** Set a follow-up date. "No rush. Can I check back with you on Thursday?" Then actually follow up on Thursday. Most sales happen on the follow-up, not the first conversation.

<SectionLabel label="Pitch Like You Mean It" />

Whether you're pitching to investors, partners, or potential customers at a meetup, you need a tight pitch.

### The Sequoia 10-Slide Format

This is the format Sequoia Capital recommends. It's been battle-tested by thousands of startups.

1. **Title** - Company name, one-line description, your name
2. **Problem** - Make them feel the pain. Use a specific story, not abstract statistics.
3. **Solution** - What you built and how it works. Show, don't tell.
4. **Why Now** - Why is this the right time? What changed? (AI capabilities, market shifts, regulation)
5. **Market Size** - How big is the opportunity? TAM, SAM, SOM.
6. **Product** - Live demo or detailed screenshots. This is where you win or lose.
7. **Business Model** - How you make money. Pricing tiers, unit economics.
8. **Traction** - Real numbers. Even small ones. 50 users, 5 paying customers, $200 MRR. Real beats projected.
9. **Team** - Why you? What have you built before? What do you know that others don't?
10. **Ask** - Be specific. "We're raising $500K to hire two engineers and reach 1,000 paying users."

### The Slides That Matter Most

**Problem** - If they don't feel the pain, nothing else matters. Use a specific person. "Sarah is a freelance designer. She spends 4 hours every week writing proposals. She hates it. She's lost 3 clients this year because her proposals were too slow."

**Traction** - Investors discount everything you say about the future. They can't discount what's already happened. "We launched 3 weeks ago. 47 users signed up. 8 are paying $19/month. Our MRR is $152." Those numbers are small. They're also real. Real beats big-but-imaginary every time.

**Ask** - Be specific about what you need and what you'll do with it. "We're raising $200K. $120K for engineering, $50K for marketing, $30K for 12 months of API costs." Vague asks get vague responses.

### AI-Assisted Deck Building

- **Gamma** - Describe your pitch in text, get a formatted deck
- **Beautiful.ai** - Templates designed for startup pitches
- **Claude + Google Slides** - Have Claude write the copy for each slide, then paste it into a clean template

Don't spend more than 2 hours on your deck. The content matters more than the design. A great story in an ugly deck beats a boring story in a beautiful deck.

### The 5-Minute Pitch Structure

| Segment | Duration | What to Cover |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 30 seconds | A surprising fact, a bold claim, or a story that grabs attention |
| Problem | 1 minute | The pain. Make it specific and relatable. |
| Solution + Demo | 1 minute | Show the product. Live demo if possible. |
| Traction | 1 minute | Numbers. Users, revenue, growth rate. |
| Ask | 30 seconds | What you want. Be specific. |
| Q&A | 1 minute | Answer directly. Don't ramble. |

<SectionLabel label="Raise or Bootstrap" />

You've got two paths. Neither is wrong. Pick the one that fits your situation.

### Bootstrap Wins When

- Your margins are good (you keep $15+ per user after API costs)
- You can grow with revenue (customers bring in more customers)
- You want full control (no board meetings, no reporting to investors)
- Your market isn't a winner-take-all race

Most AI apps should bootstrap. The tools are cheap. The distribution channels are free. You can reach profitability with a small team - or solo.

### Raise Wins When

- You're in a competitive market moving fast and speed matters more than profit
- The infrastructure is expensive (fine-tuning models, massive compute)
- You need mentorship and network access (specific industry expertise)
- You're building something that needs critical mass to work (a marketplace, a network)

### The Funding Landscape

| Source | Amount | What You Get |
|---|---|---|
| Angel investors | $25K - $100K | Capital + advice, minimal structure |
| Accelerators (YC, Antler) | $125K - $500K | Capital + mentorship + network + credibility |
| Seed VCs | $500K - $3M | Capital + board seat + strategic guidance |

**Don't give away more than 20% in your first round.** If you raise $200K and give away 30%, you've valued your company at $667K. That's hard to recover from in later rounds. 10-15% dilution for your first check is the sweet spot.

**The "I'm not raising" power move.** Investors want what they can't have. The founders who say "we're growing profitably and not actively raising" get more investor interest than the ones who send cold emails to every VC on LinkedIn. Build something real, talk about your traction publicly, and let investors come to you.

<SectionLabel label="What Happens After Day 7" />

This bootcamp is over. Your education isn't.

### The Weekly Rhythm

Ship something every week. No matter how small. A bug fix, a new feature, a design improvement, a blog post. The habit of shipping is more important than any individual feature.

Monday: plan what you'll ship this week (one thing).
Tuesday through Thursday: build it.
Friday: ship it and write about it.

That rhythm will compound faster than you expect.

### Community

Building alone is slow and lonely. Find your people.

- **Indie Hackers** - Forum full of solo founders sharing revenue numbers, lessons, and feedback
- **X/Twitter builder circles** - Follow builders who ship publicly. Reply to their posts. Share your progress.
- **Local meetups** - Search for startup or AI meetups in your city. The connections you make in person are 10x stronger than online ones.

### Follow the Builders Who Ship

Watch how these people work:
- **Pieter Levels** (@levelsio) - Built 70+ products, $3M+ ARR, shares everything publicly
- **Marc Lou** (@marc_louvion) - Shipped 20+ products, documents the entire process
- **Danny Postma** (@dannypostma) - AI product builder, shares revenue and growth tactics

Study what they build, how they market, and how they iterate. Then do the same thing in your niche.

### Revenue Targets

These are stretch goals. Not guarantees. Not promises.

| Timeline | Target | What It Means |
|---|---|---|
| Month 2 | $100 MRR | Proof someone will pay. The hardest milestone. |
| Month 6 | $1,000 MRR | A real business. You can cover some costs. |
| Year 1 | $5,000 MRR | $60K/year. Ramen profitable for many people. |

Most apps won't hit these numbers. Many won't make any money at all. That's normal. The ones that do will surprise you - they're rarely the ideas you thought were brilliant. They're the ones that solved a real problem for a specific person.

### The Honest Truth

You finished a 7-day bootcamp. You have a live app. You have users. You might even have your first paying customer.

That's more than 99% of people who "want to build an app" will ever accomplish. But it's also just the beginning.

The builders who win aren't the ones with the best ideas on Day 7. They're the ones who are still shipping on Day 70, Day 700. They're the ones who listen to their users, iterate fast, kill features that don't work, and double down on the ones that do.

You now have every tool you need. The AI handles the code. The platforms handle the infrastructure. The frameworks handle the design.

The only thing left is you, showing up every week and building.

Keep shipping.
