A washing machine that does not fit the buyer's kitchen is a bad purchase, even if the payment goes through. An assistant asked to buy one needs measurements, installation details, a delivery date, and terms for sending it back. A request such as "find a good washing machine under $800" leaves several of those decisions open. The assistant can obey the price limit and still leave a large, expensive problem at the door.

<Figure src="/images/essays/agentcommerce-purchase-checks.svg" alt="Four checks for an agent-led purchase: the buyer's requirements, product evidence, payment permission, and the item actually received" caption="A purchase needs checks before and after payment." width={800} height={400} />

Agentic commerce gives software responsibility for more of this work. An assistant might search a catalog, compare products, prepare a cart, or place an order. Some services ask for approval every time. Others offer purchases under limits set earlier. Those arrangements put different decisions in the buyer's hands, and they need different tests.

The case for using an agent rests on the purchase it produces and the effort it saves. Finding a suitable item faster is useful. So is refusing to buy when a required measurement is missing. A checkout button that works quickly can still leave the customer checking specifications, arranging a return, or arguing about a charge. Those hours belong in the account of whether the agent helped.

## Who approves the order

A recommendation leaves the purchase with the person. An agent that prepares a cart and waits for approval takes on more work, but still asks the buyer to authorize that order. An agent with advance permission can act later, perhaps when a price falls. Calling all of these "autonomous shopping" hides how much attention each requires.

| Arrangement | Software handles | Buyer decides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Advice | Search and comparison | Item and checkout |
| Approval for each order | Cart preparation and submission | Whether to approve that purchase |
| Advance permission | Later actions within agreed limits | What may be bought, when, and at what cost |

OpenAI's [Instant Checkout announcement](https://openai.com/index/buy-it-in-chatgpt/) described a September 2025 launch for US users buying single items from US Etsy sellers. Buyers confirmed the order, shipping, and payment details. The seller handled delivery, returns, and support. OpenAI's [shopping guidance](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/11128490-shopping-with-chatgpt-search) describes both in-chat checkout for eligible products and links to merchants. Its [Shopify guidance](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/20001241-shopping-from-shopify-merchants-in-chatgpt) directs customers to checkout on the seller's store. A product appearing in a conversation does not tell the buyer which route applies.

Amazon's [Alexa for Shopping announcement](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/alexa-for-shopping-ai-assistant) describes Auto Buy at a chosen price alongside actions that send a notice or add an item to a cart. A notice, a prepared cart, and a paid order require different amounts of customer involvement. Counting them together makes a service look more capable than its completed purchases establish.

Advance permission also needs a period and a quantity. A limit for one coffee order says little about the amount an agent may spend on repeated orders during a month. A buyer may approve a particular seller but reject a marketplace substitute. Permission can change before the next purchase. A service needs a way to check the current instruction rather than act on an earlier conversation that the buyer has since corrected.

These decisions affect the product itself. A person who permits automatic replacement of a familiar item may still want to approve a different brand, a used model, or a delivery surcharge. The useful permission screen exposes those choices before the agent acts. A single spending cap leaves too much of the request to interpretation.

## What a payment protocol checks

Commerce protocols describe how programs exchange product, cart, order, and payment information. They can make a transaction easier to process and easier to inspect afterward. Their documents need to be read at the level of the function they specify.

| System | Documented job | Separate question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Agentic Commerce Protocol](https://developers.openai.com/commerce/specs/checkout.md) | Checkout sessions and order updates | Does the item meet the buyer's needs? |
| [Universal Commerce Protocol](https://ucp.dev/2026-08-25/specification/overview/) | Commerce functions and version matching | Do these two services support the same functions? |
| [Agent Payments Protocol](https://ap2-protocol.org/) | Signed buying and payment permission | Do services enforce the permitted action? |
| [x402](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/x402/welcome) | Payment for a digital resource or API | Who handles a physical item's delivery and return? |

The checkout specification for ACP makes the seller responsible for the current items, shipping options, taxes, fees, and total. It describes creating, updating, completing, and cancelling a checkout session. That matters because a price in a search result can differ from the amount due when the order is ready. An agent needs the current checkout state before it commits the buyer's money.

AP2 calls its signed permission records mandates. An open mandate sets limits before the final order is known. A closed mandate records permission for a particular checkout or payment. The [UCP integration](https://ucp.dev/documentation/ucp-and-ap2/) links a payment to the agreed cart. This helps prevent permission for one order from being reused for a different order.

The checks happen in services, not through the presence of a signature on a screen. AP2's [security requirements](https://ap2-protocol.org/ap2/security_and_privacy_considerations/) assign checkout-binding checks to the merchant and signature and payment-limit checks to credential and payment services. A model's instruction to stay within a budget cannot substitute for those checks. The service accepting the request has to reject an action that exceeds the permission.

A lost connection creates another test. The merchant may accept an order before the assistant receives the reply. Retrying the same operation with the same request key should produce one order, with the seller enforcing that behavior. A demonstration needs to check the resulting orders and charges. Showing that the assistant sent a retry key proves little about what the merchant did with it.

x402 covers a different purchase, such as access to an API. A shopping agent might pay to obtain information before choosing a product. If that charge falls on the buyer, it belongs in the shopping bill even when the search ends without an order. Digital-resource payment does not settle the return terms of a washing machine purchased afterward.

## A partly correct recommendation can still be unusable

A laptop can satisfy most of a checklist and still fail the purchase. The processor may be suitable and the price acceptable, while the memory falls below a stated requirement. A score for individual requirements rewards the details the agent got right. A score for a complete request asks whether the remaining failure prevents the buyer from using the item.

Shopee researchers built [EComAgentBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17698v3) around needs spread across an opening request, a buyer profile, and scripted replies to questions. The inspected version contains 662 valid tasks. Agents can make up to 100 tool calls and ask up to ten questions. The test checks whether they obtain and use information before recommending a product.

Among seven tested models, Claude Opus 4.6 led the reported full-task accuracy measure at 57.1%, compared with 47.0% for GPT-5.4. A different measure, satisfaction of individual requirements, placed GPT-5.4 first at 81.0%, ahead of Opus at 76.6%. Choosing the headline measure changes which model leads.

<CommerceBenchmarkScores />

These runs end with a recommendation. They use constructed profiles, fixed replies, and an English-language catalog concentrated in electronics-related goods. No delivery truck arrives, and no customer has to fit the product into a kitchen. The 57.1% result describes correctness under those test rules. It cannot serve as the success rate of customers handing over their shopping.

Scoring deserves scrutiny too. An exact match to the target counts as correct without an AI judge. An alternative product can pass if the judge finds that it meets every requirement. The authors cross-checked judgments against exact matches and manually examined accepted alternatives for one model. Those checks address some grading errors without establishing perfect judging for every model. The main results table also gives point estimates rather than repeat-run variation or uncertainty intervals.

Princeton's [WebShop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.01206v4) makes a related distinction in a simulated store. Agents search, select product options, and press a simulated buy button. In its reported training comparison, further training with task rewards improved the average matching score while slightly reducing full success. That small difference does not establish that the training method generally harms performance. It shows why the partial score and the share of fully satisfied requests need separate reporting.

The testing rule has a practical consequence. A buyer may accept a different color but reject the wrong size. Required conditions and preferences need separate treatment before a run. Otherwise a grader can reward several minor matches while overlooking the requirement that made the purchase worthwhile.

## Missing information has a cost

An assistant cannot check a detail it never obtains. Product information can sit in a specification sheet, a seller's answer, a review, or a tool that charges for access. A request for the "best deal" leaves the agent to decide how much of that material to read and when to stop.

McGill researchers Wadi and Ma examine that decision in [Tool-Lab](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28372v1). Eight models choose among coffee products while details sit behind information requests. The researchers vary the stated cost of those requests and compare a vague buying goal with a precise request for the lowest price per ounce. Under costly access and vague goals, models often inspect fewer useful details, with differences among models. Clearer instructions generally preserve more of the relevant checking.

The main cost contrasts assign $10 or $50 to an information request. These are simulated charges, not fees observed in ordinary coffee shopping. The study's choice measure uses the product's price per ounce and leaves out those lookup charges. If another lookup costs more than the saving it might uncover, stopping can make sense for the buyer. A lower unit price alone does not establish a better purchase once information costs enter the bill.

For a service builder, the lesson concerns the information supplied and the goal given to the agent. Put package size beside price. Give delivery and return terms a defined place in the response. Ask for the missing measurement before choosing an appliance. Then test whether the agent used the information. Retrieving a profile or a review does not establish that its contents affected the final choice.

## The shelf still matters

MyCustomAI, Columbia, and Yale researchers use [ACES](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02630v3) to study how agents choose from a controlled store. The setting contains eight product categories with eight items in each. The authors change position, labels, prices, ratings, and review counts, and also test a text-only list of product data.

Under default buying prompts, agents respond to position and platform labels. A sponsored label reduces selection when position and other details stay fixed; an "Overall Pick" label increases it. The effects vary by model, prompt, and interface. Product-order effects persist in the text-only setting. Removing the visual storefront does not remove the ordering of the information the agent reads.

This gives merchants a reason to care about presentation even when software does the reading. It also limits what can be concluded about advertising. A paid placement might give an item a position it would otherwise never receive. A penalty for the sponsored label at a fixed position does not measure the total effect of buying that placement.

Model changes complicate the seller's task. ACES reports improvement on simple tasks with a clear answer, while also finding changes in which products agents favor. A seller cannot assume that a result obtained with one model continues after an update. Repeating the same shopping tasks can reveal whether visibility or selection changed before the business attributes a sales change to its prices or product descriptions.

Catalog access comes earlier still. OpenAI's shopping guidance says that not every available product appears and that prices can lag behind merchant updates. A small seller benefits from an assistant's search only if the assistant can find the product. Supporting checkout through an API makes an order possible once selected. It does not guarantee consideration or a favorable rank.

## Cheaper search can help a seller keep its price

[Bakos's 1997 economic model](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.43.12.1676) separates the cost of learning prices from the cost of learning product features. Making prices easier to compare can increase price competition. Helping a buyer find the closest match can instead make that seller harder to replace. A buyer who finds the exact fit may have less reason to keep searching.

These results depend on the model's assumptions about preferences, information, and sellers. They are not observations of today's AI assistants. They do explain why making search cheaper is insufficient grounds for predicting a lower bill. An assistant can improve product matching without creating the same pressure on prices as a tool that makes competing offers easy to compare.

[Ursu's hotel-ranking dissertation](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/w2hvk-p7722/files/Ursu_uchicago_0330D_13283.pdf?download=1) examines field data with randomized result order. Hotels in higher positions receive more clicks and bookings in the released sample. Among clicked hotels, the analysis finds no detectable association between position and booking conversion. Because ranking affects clicking, that selected subset is not a separate randomized test of what happens after a click.

The data also exclude searches with no click and oversample bookings. Their rates cannot describe all Expedia visitors. The finding concerns human hotel searches, not AI buying, but it gives a useful reason to distinguish getting considered from getting chosen after inspection.

To measure an assistant's effect on competition, record the products it could reach, the products it showed, the prices offered, and the resulting orders. Keep stock and fees in the comparison. Similar defaults could send many buyers toward a few products; wider access could help overlooked sellers. Simulated choices alone cannot establish either market result.

## Accepting advice does not authorize spending

Consumer research gives different answers depending on the decision people face. In [Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey's forecasting experiments](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000033), seeing an imperfect algorithm perform can reduce willingness to choose it, even when it outperforms the human comparison. Participants react to observed mistakes before selecting a forecaster for later predictions.

[Logg, Minson, and Moore](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2018.12.005) test another decision. In key experiments, people receive identical advice labeled as coming from either a person or an algorithm. They often place more weight on algorithm-labeled advice. That preference weakens when the comparison concerns their own judgment and among forecasting experts. Advice weighting and forecaster choice after errors do not form one measure of trust.

The [follow-up control experiments](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2016.2643) by Dietvorst and colleagues find greater model use when people can change a forecast, even within a narrow range. More reliance on the model improves performance in those tasks. That does not mean each manual adjustment helps. Nor does it establish that an approval button improves the quality of a shopping order.

For shopping, control has to be specific enough to use. A buyer needs to see which item, total price, delivery commitment, and return conditions they are approving. Asking for consent after burying those details in a long conversation creates a checkbox without showing how carefully the buyer checked the order. Measure the checking time and the errors that survive approval.

[De Bellis and Johar's review of autonomous shopping](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2019.12.004) also describes reasons people may retain parts of the process. Choosing can express identity, provide enjoyment, or involve someone else. Delegating the replacement of a familiar household item need not imply wanting an agent to choose a gift. These are reasons to test permission by task, rather than make one broad claim about consumers accepting AI.

## Keep the survey questions attached to the percentages

Riskified's releases illustrate how easily different questions turn into an apparent adoption story. Both surveys cover people aged 18 to 64 who bought online recently, but their country coverage differs.

| Survey | Sample | Reported answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [September-October 2025](https://www.riskified.com/press/global-study-73-of-shoppers-using-ai-in-shopping-journey-but-merchants-face-new-agentic-commerce-risks/) | 5,400 people across nine markets | 73% used AI while shopping; 13% bought after an AI referral; 70% were at least somewhat comfortable with an agent buying |
| [Q1 2026 edition](https://www.riskified.com/press/riskified-agentic-commerce-pulse/) | 2,000 people in the US and UK | 61.5% used AI for discovery or recommendations; 55.0% were uncomfortable with AI buying for them |

The country mix changes, and the releases do not supply complete questionnaires and matched response options. Subtracting the totals cannot establish a change in the same population's trust. The share reporting discomfort also does not establish that everyone else felt comfortable; an unreported response category may sit between those positions.

A purchase after an AI referral can end in ordinary human checkout. It says nothing by itself about whether an agent chose the item, submitted payment, or handled delivery. Within the 2026 survey, the published totals do not reveal exactly how advice users overlap with people reluctant to delegate buying. Treating those figures as a count of autonomous orders invents information the survey does not provide.

Riskified sells fraud-prevention services. Its releases provide useful descriptions of the samples and answers, but not the weighting details or individual responses needed for a deeper check. Those limits matter more than attaching a large market forecast to the percentages. Product discovery, willingness to delegate, and completed paid orders need their own records.

## An attack can succeed before the payment

A product listing is input to a shopping agent. An attacker who controls that listing can place instructions inside the material the agent reads, trying to make it favor an item or misuse a tool. AP2's security document includes agents themselves in the threat model. Checking payment permission does not remove the need to check the actions that produce the proposed order.

ETH Zurich and Invariant Labs' [AgentDojo](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13352v3) pairs attacker goals with user tasks in controlled tool environments. Its 629 security cases reuse 97 user tasks. The benchmark measures task completion without attacks, attacker-goal completion, and successful user-task completion without adversarial side effects under attack. An agent that completes the requested booking and also leaks information has not met that last criterion.

Low attacker success can be misleading if an agent lacks the ability to carry out either goal. Defenses need a usefulness measure too. In AgentDojo's GPT-4o tests, some defenses reduce attacks while improving ordinary task completion. The attack detector has false alarms that block valid work. Restricting tools helps when an attack needs a forbidden action, but can fail when an allowed tool is enough to cause the harm.

[Whispers of Wealth](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22569v2) tests a much smaller AP2-style shopping prototype. Four Gemini-2.5-Flash agents process synthetic data around one basketball-shoe request. The researchers repeat the baseline, product-description attack, and credential attack ten times each. The attacker's product ranks first in all ten product-attack runs. The authors judge six to conflict clearly with the request and describe ambiguity in the others. Synthetic cross-account exposure occurs in two of ten credential-attack runs.

Those are repetitions of one task, not thirty independent shoppers. The study does not break cryptographic signatures or compromise real customer accounts. Its older mandate terminology also differs from the current AP2 documentation. It supplies examples of prototype failure paths, not a fraud rate or evidence that every AP2 implementation has the same flaw.

Payment services need to enforce amounts and recipients; tools need to enforce account access. Test how the service handles a buyer changing or withdrawing future permission. Attempt an inappropriate action and inspect what the service allows, including whether a defense prevents an ordinary, permitted purchase.

## The customer still has a contract

Electronic agents do not place every purchase outside existing law. The US [E-SIGN Act](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/7001) protects certain contracts from losing effect solely because electronic agents were involved. Section 7001(h) conditions that protection on the action being legally attributable to the person bound. It does not automatically make every agent action the user's action, and subsection (b) preserves other rights and duties.

Washington's [automated-transaction provision](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=1.80.130) offers a state-law example of contract formation through electronic agents. Its [error provision](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=1.80.090) provides conditional relief for a person's error when dealing with another party's agent. Conditions include lack of an opportunity to prevent or correct the error, prompt notice, and return and non-use requirements. A general right to reverse any unwanted AI purchase does not follow from that text.

For covered online goods purchases from traders, [EU consumers generally have 14 days after delivery to withdraw](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/consumers/shopping/returns/index_en.htm). Exceptions include perishable or customized goods; dated travel and some digital content have different rules. The general right excludes private-seller purchases. AI disclosure duties under [Article 50](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-50) address another issue and do not settle who pays for a mistaken order.

These rules make the order record useful. The buyer needs the seller, the item, the approved total and terms, the instruction that authorized the purchase, and a route to support. Keep requested, ordered, and received details separate. A seller sending the wrong product and an agent ordering the wrong product can leave the buyer with the same box, but they are different failures.

[Busch's consumer-law analysis](https://doi.org/10.1017/glj.2026.10188) examines the earlier moment when people set an agent's goals and permissions. It distinguishes manipulation of an agent by outside parties from manipulation by the provider operating the agent. The argument supports examining defaults, paid recommendations, and the permission screen before the first order exists. Its reform proposals do not create additional binding duties.

## Count the work after checkout

A useful purchase test can compare ordinary shopping, AI advice, approval for each order, and advance permission on matched tasks and spending limits. State required conditions and acceptable substitutes beforehand, and record preferences separately. First-delivery success means receiving an item that meets those rules. Checkout completion is one event on the way there.

<CommerceOutcomeChecks />

Keep all attempts in the accounting, including abandoned searches, failed payments, duplicate orders, and unresolved returns. A service that reports only completed orders leaves out the work spent on its failures. A service that counts every refusal as a failure misses cases where the requested purchase is impossible or unauthorized. Define those cases before running the comparison, rather than relabel an inconvenient result afterward.

Record buyer-paid charges net of refunds, with checking and correction time alongside them. A refunded mistake is still a wrong first order. Record the stage of failure and the reason for a return instead of treating every refund or delivery problem as a model error. A buyer changing their mind after a suitable delivery differs from an agent ignoring an agreed requirement.

Tests with a shared catalog hold product access fixed. Comparisons of actual services also need to record which sellers and products each service can reach. Repeat tasks under changed model versions and attacks, while distinguishing different tasks from reruns of the same task. [WebArena's unachievable tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13854v4) offer one example of treating a justified stop as an outcome worth checking.

The spending limit, the permission record, and the receipt all answer useful questions. The washing machine still has to fit. Before calling the agent a time-saver, count the minutes the buyer spent getting the wrong machine out of the house.
