A washing machine that does not fit your kitchen is a bad purchase, even when the payment goes through cleanly. If you ask an assistant to buy one, it needs your measurements, installation details, a delivery window, and clear terms for sending it back. A prompt like "find a good washing machine under $800" leaves most of those choices blank. The software can hit your price target to the penny and still drop a three-hundred-pound problem on your doorstep.

<Figure src="/images/essays/agentcommerce-purchase-checks.svg" alt="Four checks for an agent-led purchase: the buyer's requirements, product evidence, payment permission, and the item actually received" caption="A purchase needs checks before and after payment." width={800} height={400} />

Agentic commerce hands more of this pipeline to software. An assistant searches catalogs, compares items, builds carts, or completes checkout. Some services ask you to approve every transaction. Others execute purchases within spending limits you set in advance. Each setup asks something different of the buyer, and each needs a different test.

The case for using a shopping agent rests on what arrives at your door and how much effort you save. Finding a suitable product faster helps. Refusing to place an order when a critical dimension is missing helps even more. But a checkout button that works in two seconds does nothing for you if you spend the next three days measuring doors, printing return labels, and disputing freight charges. Those hours belong on the ledger when we ask whether the software actually did its job.

## Who approves the order

Recommendation tools leave the transaction to you. An agent that builds a cart and waits for your confirmation takes on more work, but you still make the financial call. An agent with advance permission acts on its own, perhaps when an item drops below a target price. Calling all three "autonomous shopping" blurs the attention each requires.

| Arrangement | Software handles | Buyer decides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Advice | Search and comparison | Item selection and checkout |
| Per-order approval | Cart preparation and submission | Whether to approve the specific purchase |
| Advance permission | Execution within agreed limits | What may be bought, when, and under what conditions |

When OpenAI launched [Instant Checkout](https://openai.com/index/buy-it-in-chatgpt/) in September 2025 for US Etsy sellers, buyers still reviewed the item, shipping address, and payment method before confirming. The merchant handled delivery, returns, and support. OpenAI's [shopping guidance](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/11128490-shopping-with-chatgpt-search) mixes in-chat checkout with regular store links, while its [Shopify guidance](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/20001241-shopping-from-shopify-merchants-in-chatgpt) routes buyers directly to the merchant site. A product card appearing in a chat window tells you little about which checkout path you will actually take.

Amazon's [Alexa for Shopping](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/alexa-for-shopping-ai-assistant) pairs Auto Buy at a chosen price ceiling with notifications and cart additions. A price alert, an item dropped into a cart, and an automated payment demand completely different levels of human involvement. Counting them together makes an assistant look far more autonomous than its real purchase records prove.

Advance permission also demands boundaries on time and volume. A $50 allowance for one bag of coffee does not mean the agent may spend $50 every week. You might permit a direct manufacturer sale but reject a third-party marketplace seller. You might also update your mind tomorrow. An agent needs to verify active instructions against your current preferences, not dredge up an old chat prompt you corrected days ago.

These boundaries dictate product fit. If you authorize an agent to restock laundry detergent, you probably still want a say before it switches brands, buys refurbished stock, or accepts an expensive express shipping fee. A good permission interface puts those boundaries upfront. A simple dollar cap leaves far too much to chance.

## What a payment protocol checks

Commerce protocols standardize how applications exchange product details, carts, orders, and payment tokens. They make transactions simpler to run and audit. But reading their technical specifications shows where their duties stop.

| System | Documented job | Separate question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Agentic Commerce Protocol](https://developers.openai.com/commerce/specs/checkout.md) | Checkout sessions and order updates | Does the item fit the buyer's home and needs? |
| [Universal Commerce Protocol](https://ucp.dev/2026-08-25/specification/overview/) | Commerce functions and version negotiation | Do both systems support the same transaction features? |
| [Agent Payments Protocol](https://ap2-protocol.org/) | Signed buyer and payment mandates | Does the server enforce the authorized boundaries? |
| [x402](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/x402/welcome) | Payments for digital resources and APIs | Who takes responsibility for delivery and returns? |

In OpenAI and Stripe's Agentic Commerce Protocol (ACP), the merchant owns the cart state: items, shipping methods, taxes, surcharges, and total price. ACP governs creating, updating, completing, and cancelling that checkout session. This matters because catalog prices frequently change before an order finalizes. An agent must confirm the live total from the merchant before committing funds.

Google's Agent Payments Protocol (AP2) centers on signed permission records called mandates. An open mandate sets spending boundaries before an order exists. A closed mandate locks in permission for a specific checkout session and merchant. Integrating AP2 with the [Universal Commerce Protocol (UCP)](https://ucp.dev/documentation/ucp-and-ap2/) cryptographically ties the payment to the exact items in the cart. This prevents an authorization meant for one order from being applied to another.

These security boundaries live in server-side checks, not visual badges. AP2's [security specifications](https://ap2-protocol.org/ap2/security_and_privacy_considerations/) require merchants to verify cart bindings, while payment providers verify digital signatures and spending ceilings. Prompting a language model to "stay under budget" cannot replace backend enforcement. The service receiving the request must reject any charge that exceeds the mandate.

Network drops introduce another failure point. A merchant server might capture an order just as the connection drops, leaving the assistant without an acknowledgment. Retrying the call with an idempotency key ensures you get billed once, but only if the merchant enforces that key. Testing an agent requires auditing actual charges and order receipts, not just observing that the assistant sent a retry header.

Protocols like x402 solve a different problem: paying for digital resources such as paid APIs or paywalled documents via HTTP status code 402. Donated to the Linux Foundation in April 2026 with backing from major cloud providers, card networks, and payment processors, x402 handles machine-to-machine micropayments using stablecoins. An agent might pay a small fraction of a cent to pull technical specifications or inventory data while researching products. If you get billed for that query, it belongs in your shopping costs even if the search ends without an order. But a completed micro-settlement for an API call settles nothing about whether a delivered physical appliance can be returned.

Traditional payment networks approach the problem from the opposite direction. Rather than building new currencies, Visa and Mastercard have built frameworks (such as Visa Trusted Agent Protocol and Mastercard Agent Pay) that wrap existing card rails with dynamic agent tokens, merchant bindings, and spending ceilings. Yet these networks face the same structural boundary: verifying that a card token is valid or that an agent is authenticated does not tell the merchant whether the item fits inside your house. As central bank analysts noted in late 2026, delegating a purchase shifts the core question of payments from "is this the cardholder?" to "did this agent possess the authority to execute this exact intent?" Proving the transaction was authorized still leaves open whether the purchase was correct.

## A partly correct recommendation can still be unusable

A laptop can match nine out of ten items on your checklist and still be useless. The processor can be fast and the price fair, but if the memory is soldered at 8GB when you need 32GB for your work, the purchase fails. Scoring an agent on individual requirement matches rewards partial credit. Scoring it on the complete task tests whether you can actually use what it bought.

Shopee's [EComAgentBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17698v3) tests this across 662 tasks where buyer preferences are split across an initial prompt, a persona profile, and scripted conversational replies. Agents can run up to 100 tool calls and ask up to 10 questions to gather information before suggesting a product.

When tested across seven leading models, Claude Opus 4.6 took the top spot in full-task accuracy at 57.1%, while GPT-5.4 reached 47.0%. Yet on the separate rubric score, which counts how many individual preferences the model met, GPT-5.4 led at 81.0% against Opus at 76.6%. The benchmark winner flips depending on whether you demand a fully usable match or reward partial overlap.

<CommerceBenchmarkScores />

These benchmark runs conclude with a product recommendation. They use artificial buyer personas, scripted answers, and an English-language catalog concentrated in consumer electronics. No courier delivers an item, and no buyer tries to fit it into a room. A 57.1% success rate measures adherence to specific benchmark rules, not how often an agent delivers what real customers need.

Grading methods need the same scrutiny. Exact matches to target products pass automatically, but alternative picks rely on an LLM judge to verify that every requirement was satisfied. The authors validated these verdicts against exact matches and hand-checked one model's alternatives, which catches obvious grading mistakes without guaranteeing reliable evaluations across all models. The published tables also report single point estimates without repeat-run variance or confidence intervals.

Princeton's [WebShop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.01206v4) uncovered a similar pattern in simulated stores, where agents search, select options, and click a purchase button. In reinforcement learning experiments, tuning models with reward functions improved their average attribute match score while slightly reducing their rate of complete success. That does not mean reward tuning breaks an agent, but it demonstrates why average feature scores and full-task completion rates must be reported side by side.

This distinction has practical consequences for buyers. You might tolerate a navy jacket instead of black, but you cannot wear shoes two sizes too small. Crucial constraints and loose preferences must be separated upfront. Without that split, an evaluation can praise an agent for matching color and brand while missing the one requirement that made the purchase worthwhile.

## Missing information has a cost

An assistant cannot evaluate data it never reads. Critical details hide inside specification sheets, seller Q&As, user reviews, or paid research tools. A prompt asking for the "best deal" leaves the agent to guess how deeply to search before stopping.

In [Tool-Lab](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28372v1), McGill researchers Wadi and Ma tested this trade-off. They had eight models choose ground coffee while placing key specifications behind tool lookups. When they attached simulated fees of $10 or $50 to each lookup, models faced with vague shopping goals cut their tool queries short, skipping important product details. Clear prompts, such as minimizing price per ounce, kept models searching more consistently.

Those $10 and $50 request fees were experimental levers, not real-world lookup costs. Furthermore, the benchmark calculated final product efficiency strictly on price per ounce, ignoring the accumulated lookup charges. In practice, if an extra query costs more than the discount it uncovers, stopping early is the rational choice. Finding a lower item price only helps if it does not cost more to find than it saves at checkout.

For anyone engineering these systems, the takeaway is straightforward: put key details directly in front of the agent. Place package weight beside list price. Surface delivery fees and return terms in the primary response. Force the model to collect missing measurements before suggesting an appliance. Then verify that the retrieved data actually guided the decision. Pulling a specification into context does not mean the model factored it into the cart.

## The shelf still matters

Researchers from MyCustomAI, Columbia, and Yale tested product presentation using [ACES](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02630v3), evaluating how agents shop in a controlled eight-category store with eight items per category. They adjusted product ranking, labels, prices, ratings, and review counts, alongside a plain text-only feed.

Given standard buying prompts, agents favored top positions and platform badges. Adding a "Sponsored" label lowered an item's selection rate when position and price were held steady; an "Overall Pick" badge increased it. The magnitude of these shifts varied across models, prompts, and interfaces. Position bias remained strong in the text-only layout: removing the visual UI did not eliminate the ranking advantage of items listed first in the prompt.

Sellers must still care about catalog rank even when software handles the reading. But this also shows why treating sponsored tags in isolation can be misleading. An ad fee might lift an obscure item to the top of the feed, giving it visibility it would otherwise lack. Measuring the drop caused by a "Sponsored" tag at an identical rank does not reflect the net benefit of buying placement.

Model updates add more volatility. ACES found that newer models solved simple product lookups more accurately, but their underlying product preferences drifted between releases. A merchant cannot assume that a listing favored by one model version will stay on top after an update. Running regular benchmark tasks is the only way to tell whether a drop in orders comes from price sensitivity or a quiet shift in model preference.

Access comes before ranking. OpenAI's documentation notes that its shopping tools do not index every merchant item, and displayed prices can lag behind live store updates. A merchant only gains from agentic search if the agent can discover its inventory. Having an open checkout API lets an agent complete an order, but it does nothing to get your product noticed in the first place.

Commercial access can also be revoked overnight. Even as industry groups launch open standards like UCP and MPP, the largest retail platforms actively police their perimeters. Major marketplaces have moved aggressively in court and at the edge to block unauthorized buyer agents: federal courts granted preliminary injunctions against third-party agent browsers scraping product catalogs, and major retail platforms have systematically blocked browser-based shopping agents deployed by social networks. Having a clever buying model or an open payment token does you no good if the store's servers block your IP before you can read the catalog.

## Cheaper search can help a seller keep its price

In 1997, economist [J. Yannis Bakos modeled](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.43.12.1676) what happens when consumers can search markets with less effort. He separated the cost of comparing prices from the cost of learning whether a product fits the buyer's needs. Lowering price-search costs intensifies price competition among sellers. But lowering feature-search costs helps buyers find products tailored to their exact preferences, making competing options less attractive substitutes and allowing sellers to protect their margins.

Bakos worked from theoretical assumptions about consumer preferences and seller competition, not modern language models. Yet his distinction explains why cheaper automated search does not automatically cut consumer bills. An assistant that finds a product matching your taste can reduce your incentive to keep hunting for bargains elsewhere.

Raluca Ursu's [empirical study of hotel searches](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/w2hvk-p7722/files/Ursu_uchicago_0330D_13283.pdf?download=1) shows how position shapes decisions. Using Expedia data with randomized hotel rankings, she found that higher positions received substantially more clicks and bookings. But among hotels that received a click, search position showed no detectable relationship with booking conversion. Consumers clicked top results first, but once they evaluated the property details, quality and price drove the final choice.

Ursu analyzed human hotel bookings, not AI agents, and her dataset oversampled completed reservations while excluding clickless sessions. Even so, the lesson translates directly to agentic commerce: getting considered by an agent and getting selected after inspection are two distinct steps.

To evaluate how shopping agents influence retail competition, we must track the entire pipeline: which items the assistant can access, which ones it reviews, the prices it compares, and the final orders it places. Shipping fees and stock levels must be factored in. Uniform agent defaults could funnel market demand toward a few dominant sellers, while broader discovery tools could give independent merchants a chance. Simulated runs alone cannot settle which outcome will dominate.

## Accepting advice does not authorize spending

Consumer psychology shows that trusting an algorithm with an opinion differs from trusting it with your bank account. In [forecasting experiments](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000033) by Berkeley Dietvorst, Joseph Simmons, and Cade Massey, watching an algorithm make an error sharply reduced participants' willingness to rely on it, even when the algorithm consistently beat human alternatives. People hold machines to stricter standards after witnessing a failure.

Jennifer Logg, Julia Minson, and Don Moore [tested algorithmic advice](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2018.12.005) from another angle. When given identical recommendations labeled as coming from either a human or an algorithm, participants leaned more heavily on the algorithmic advice. That preference faded when comparing the advice to their own judgment, and professional forecasters showed much less algorithmic reliance. How people weight advice and whether they abandon a system after an error are two different behaviors.

In [follow-up experiments](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2016.2643), Dietvorst and colleagues found that allowing users to modify a model's prediction, even within narrow boundaries, significantly increased their willingness to use it. More reliance on the model improved overall task performance. But that finding does not mean every human tweak improved the forecast, nor does it prove that adding an approval screen makes a shopping agent order better products.

In shopping, control must be actionable. Buyers need to see the exact product, total charge, delivery window, and return terms before funds transfer. Asking someone to click "Approve" after burying those details inside a long conversational thread provides legal cover without ensuring real oversight. We need to track how long buyers spend reviewing orders and how many mistakes slip past their approval.

In their [review of autonomous shopping](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2019.12.004), Romain de Bellis and Gita Johar emphasize that people retain certain shopping tasks because they enjoy browsing, want to express their taste, or are buying gifts for loved ones. Allowing an agent to restock paper towels does not mean a buyer wants it picking out anniversary presents. Buying autonomy must be tested category by category, rather than assumed from broad surveys on AI adoption.

## Keep the survey questions attached to the percentages

Vendor surveys often blend different questions into an aggressive adoption narrative. Riskified published two studies on online shoppers aged 18 to 64, but their sample compositions and questions differed.

| Survey | Sample | Reported findings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [September-October 2025](https://www.riskified.com/press/global-study-73-of-shoppers-using-ai-in-shopping-journey-but-merchants-face-new-agentic-commerce-risks/) | 5,400 respondents across nine global markets | 73% used AI while shopping; 13% bought after an AI referral; 70% were at least somewhat comfortable with an agent buying |
| [Q1 2026 edition](https://www.riskified.com/press/riskified-agentic-commerce-pulse/) | 2,000 respondents in the US and UK | 61.5% used AI for discovery or recommendations; 55.0% were uncomfortable with AI buying on their behalf |

The geographic footprint shifted from nine international markets to two Western countries, and the published materials do not include identical question wordings or full answer distributions. Subtracting one percentage from another does not prove that consumer sentiment turned negative over time. Furthermore, reporting that 55.0% felt uncomfortable delegating purchases does not imply the remaining 45.0% were ready to do so; neutral responses often sit between those extremes.

A purchase that starts with an AI recommendation usually ends with standard human checkout. It tells us nothing about whether an agent selected the item, authorized payment, or coordinated delivery. Within the 2026 survey, published summaries do not show how many consumers using AI for search would also allow it to spend their money. Citing those figures as evidence of autonomous purchasing invents claims the data cannot back.

Riskified sells fraud prevention tools, and its reports offer interesting data points about consumer attitudes. But without open microdata, sampling weights, and question trees, those numbers cannot support sweeping market forecasts. Product discovery, buyer willingness, and automated purchases must be tracked as three separate activities.

## An attack can succeed before the payment

A merchant listing is untrusted input. An attacker who controls a product description can hide prompt injection instructions designed to manipulate an agent's reasoning or hijack its tools. AP2's official threat model explicitly treats agent software as a compromised surface. Validating payment tokens does nothing to verify the actions that produced the order in the first place.

In [AgentDojo](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13352v3), researchers from ETH Zurich and Invariant Labs tested these vulnerabilities across 629 security scenarios built on 97 user tasks. The benchmark tracks three outcomes: task completion without attacks, attacker success rates, and user task completion without side effects under attack. An assistant that successfully books a flight while quietly exfiltrating customer data fails the security test.

A low attacker success rate can be misleading if the model is too weak to follow either user or attacker instructions. Security defenses must be measured alongside utility. In AgentDojo's tests with GPT-4o, some defensive prompts reduced attack rates while helping standard completion. But automated attack detectors produced false positives that blocked valid purchases. Restricting tool permissions stopped attacks that depended on forbidden calls, but offered zero protection when allowed tools were sufficient to cause harm.

The [Whispers of Wealth](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22569v2) study tested a smaller AP2-style prototype using synthetic data. Four Gemini-2.5-Flash agents processed a request for basketball shoes under clean conditions, description-injection attacks, and credential-theft attempts, running each test ten times. The injected product claimed the top recommendation in all ten attack runs, with six cases violating user constraints. Synthetic cross-account credential leaks occurred in two out of ten attempts.

These runs repeated a single shopping prompt ten times; they were not a trial of thirty independent buyers. The authors did not break cryptographic signatures or access live consumer accounts, and their prototype used an early draft of AP2 terminology. The study demonstrates real attack paths in experimental code, but it does not establish a global fraud rate or prove that production AP2 implementations are broken.

Payment gateways must enforce strict dollar limits and merchant restrictions, while tools must lock down account permissions. Services must also handle permission changes cleanly when a buyer revokes access. When testing security, submit unauthorized requests to see what the server blocks, and verify whether defensive filters disrupt legitimate purchases.

## The customer still has a contract

Delegating a purchase to software does not exempt the transaction from commercial law. Under the US [E-SIGN Act](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/7001), contracts cannot be denied legal validity solely because an electronic agent was used. Section 7001(h) applies this protection only when the agent's act can be legally attributed to the person being bound. It does not declare every output of an agent to be an authorized act of the user, and Section 7001(b) preserves all underlying legal rights and duties.

State commercial statutes provide practical boundaries. Washington's [Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=1.80.130) recognizes contracts formed by automated agents. Its [error-defense provision](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=1.80.090) gives buyers conditional protection when they make an honest mistake interacting with an automated system. But relief applies only if the system offered no opportunity to catch the mistake, the buyer sent prompt notice, and the goods remained unused. The statute does not give consumers a blanket right to cancel any unwanted AI transaction.

European rules place different obligations on sellers. For online purchases from registered traders, [EU consumers generally have 14 days to withdraw](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/consumers/shopping/returns/index_en.htm) after delivery. Certain goods, like custom items and perishable food, are exempt, while transport bookings and digital media carry separate provisions. Private sales between individuals fall outside the rule. Article 50 of the [EU AI Act](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-50) requires platforms to inform users when they interact with AI, but disclosure rules do not resolve who pays for a bad order.

These statutes make comprehensive audit trails essential. A buyer needs to inspect the merchant identity, product specifications, authorized price, mandate timestamp, and support channels. System logs must distinguish between what was requested, what was ordered, and what was delivered. A seller dispatching the wrong box and an agent ordering the wrong SKU leave the buyer with the same headache, but the legal remedies are completely different.

Legal scholar [Christoph Busch argues](https://doi.org/10.1017/glj.2026.10188) that consumer protection must focus on the moment permissions and prompts are set. He distinguishes third-party prompt injection from provider-level steering, where an assistant platform quietly favors affiliated merchants or profitable listings. His analysis shows why system defaults, paid placements, and mandate screens must be audited before an order occurs. While these proposals are legal arguments rather than binding laws, they highlight the real pressure points in agent design.

## Count the work after checkout

An informative trial of shopping agents should compare regular browsing, conversational advice, per-order approval, and advance permission on identical tasks and budgets. Hard constraints and flexible preferences must be set in advance. The true test of success is receiving an item that satisfies those requirements on the first delivery. Clicking checkout is just one milestone along the way.

Every shopping session belongs in the evaluation data: aborted searches, rejected payments, duplicate shipments, and ongoing returns. A benchmark that counts only successful checkouts hides the friction generated by its failures. Conversely, an agent that stops and asks for clarification when a constraint cannot be met should be credited with a correct decision, not penalized as a failure. Those conditions must be documented before running tests, not rationalized after seeing poor numbers.

True shopping expenses must include net charges after refunds, alongside the time buyers spend verifying specifications and fixing mistakes. Getting a refund does not turn an unusable delivery into a good experience. Evaluations must document exactly where a transaction broke down, distinguishing a customer changing their mind from an assistant ignoring an explicit constraint.

Shared-catalog benchmarks hold product availability constant. But tests of commercial products must also log which stores and inventory each agent can reach. Tests should be repeated across model updates and adversarial attacks, keeping duplicate tasks distinct from fresh ones. As benchmarks like [WebArena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13854v4) demonstrate, knowing when to stop is just as important as knowing how to buy.

Spending caps, digital signatures, and merchant receipts all answer technical questions. But the washing machine still has to fit through your door. Before calling an agent a breakthrough in efficiency, count how many minutes you spent hauling the wrong one back onto the truck.
