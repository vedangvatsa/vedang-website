## The current picture

For the seven days to 12 August 2026, 35.6 percent of HTTP requests on Cloudflare's global network came from bots, not humans. The remaining 64.4 percent was human traffic. The figure comes from [Cloudflare Radar](https://radar.cloudflare.com/bots), which processes data from Cloudflare's network of 330 cities serving an average of 81 million HTTP requests per second.

The United States originated 42.6 percent of all bot traffic. Germany was second at 5.9 percent, the Netherlands third at 4.7 percent. By autonomous system, Microsoft originated 9 percent, Amazon Web Services 14.7 percent across two AS numbers, and Google 8.5 percent. Three cloud providers generate nearly a third of the world's bot traffic. The bulk of AI crawler traffic runs on their infrastructure.

The 35.6 percent figure is lower than the widely cited statistic that bots account for more than half of all web traffic. That number comes from Imperva's [2016 Bad Bot Report](https://www.imperva.com/blog/bot-traffic-report-2016/), which examined 16.7 billion visits to 100,000 randomly selected domains and found that 52 percent of web traffic was automated. The data has been cited as evidence for the [dead internet theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Internet_theory) since the theory's popularisation in 2021. Imperva's [2023 report](https://www.imperva.com/resources/resource-library/reports/2023-imperva-bad-bot-report/) found 49.6 percent automated traffic, a 2 percent rise from 2022 that the report partly attributed to AI models scraping the web for training content.

Cloudflare's figure is smaller because the company actively mitigates malicious bot traffic. Cloudflare mitigates approximately 6 percent of global traffic as potentially malicious. Its free-tier AI crawler blocking feature, [launched in July 2024](https://blog.cloudflare.com/declaring-your-aindependence-block-ai-bots-scrapers-and-crawlers-with-a-single-click/), has been adopted by millions of websites. The combination of mitigation and blocking reduces the observed bot share relative to raw internet traffic.

The composition of the 35.6 percent matters more than the total. Most bot traffic is not AI crawlers. The [Cloudflare 2025 Year in Review](https://blog.cloudflare.com/radar-2025-year-in-review/), covering through December 2025, found that search engine crawlers generated 40 percent of verified bot traffic, AI crawlers 20 percent, and search engine optimisation bots 13 percent. Googlebot alone, which crawls for both search indexing and AI training, originated more than a quarter of all verified bot traffic and 4.5 percent of HTML requests. All other AI bots combined generated 4.2 percent.

## The dead internet, re-examined

The dead internet theory was first posted in January 2021 by a user named IlluminatiPirate on the forum Agora Road's Macintosh Cafe. It argued that the internet had become a wasteland of automated content manipulated by algorithmic curation at the hands of coordinated state actors. The theory spread through YouTube, Reddit, and Twitter before entering mainstream coverage with a [September 2021 article in The Atlantic](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2021/08/dead-internet-theory-online-community-future/619937/) by Kaitlyn Tiffany.

In 2024, Yoshija Walter published a piece in [AI and Society](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-023-01857-0) arguing that "the now discussed and once speculative theory" was increasingly observable as AI-generated content proliferated across social platforms. In 2026, Hal Berghel published a paper in [Computer](https://doi.org/10.1109/MC.2025.3616665) distinguishing between the full conspiratorial version and a "leaner" dead internet theory "stripped of paranoia, prejudice, politics and polemic." Berghel identified algorithmically generated content, generative AI byproducts, and the resulting mistrust as the core phenomena, and argued that dismissing the core criticisms because conspiracy theorists attach implausible claims to them would be "a mistake of the first order."

The term has been adopted colloquially. In May 2025, [Bloomberg published](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-05-08/maybe-ai-slop-is-killing-the-internet-after-all) "Maybe AI Slop Is Killing the Internet, After All," noting that the arrival of tools such as ChatGPT and Midjourney had made the theory "look downright prophetic." In September 2025, [Time magazine asked](https://time.com/720192/dead-internet-theory-ai/) "What to Know About the 'Dead Internet' Theory and Why It's Spreading," documenting the theory's shift from fringe conspiracy to mainstream discussion. Later that month, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman [posted on X](https://x.com/sama/status/1833901157138768035): "i never took the dead internet theory that seriously but it seems like there are really a lot of LLM-run twitter accounts now." In October 2025, [Forbes reported](https://www.forbes.com/sites/conormurray/2025/10/25/ohanian-and-altman-warn-of-dead-internet-theory/) on Ohanian and Altman warning of dead internet theory, framing the question: "Is AI Making It Happen?" In June 2025, Reddit co-founder Alexis Ohanian said he had "long subscribed to the dead internet theory" since AI began passing Turing tests. At TechCrunch Disrupt in October 2025, he told Digg co-founder Kevin Rose "the dead internet theory is real." Digg, which relaunched in January 2026 and shut down two months later due to what it called an "unprecedented bot problem," [rebranded itself in May 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/11/digg-relaunches-as-ai-news-aggregator/) as an AI news aggregator, stating on its homepage that bots were the reason for the pivot.

In December 2024, Meta announced plans to introduce AI-powered autonomous accounts on Facebook and Instagram. Connor Hayes, vice-president of product for generative AI at Meta, told the [Financial Times](https://www.ft.com/content/91184c4e-c6e1-4bb9-b7e5-4e4abef1685c) that these AI characters "will have bios and profile pictures and be able to generate and share content powered by AI on the platform." The accounts were quickly removed after user backlash. The same year, AI-generated images on Facebook known as "AI slop," including thousands of variations of "Shrimp Jesus," went viral. [The Guardian described](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2024/may/19/spam-junk-slop-the-latest-wave-of-ai-behind-the-zombie-internet) the phenomenon as "the zombie internet," and [NBC News reported](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/social-media/facebook-users-say-amen-bizarre-ai-generated-images-jesus-rcna143836) that Facebook users were engaging with these images by the thousands, posting "Amen" in comments under AI-generated depictions of Christ merged with shellfish.

## How crawlers identify themselves

Every major AI company publishes documentation for its crawlers. [OpenAI lists four](https://platform.openai.com/docs/bots): GPTBot for training, OAI-SearchBot for search, ChatGPT-User for user-driven visits, and OAI-AdsBot for ad verification. [Google publishes](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawlers-fetchers/google-common-crawlers) Googlebot for search and AI training combined, plus Google-Extended as an opt-out token for Gemini training and grounding. [Perplexity lists](https://docs.perplexity.ai/guides/bots) PerplexityBot for search and Perplexity-User for user-driven visits. Anthropic's crawler documentation is behind a support page.

Web crawlers identify themselves to servers through the user-agent field of an HTTP request, a [protocol that has operated informally since 1994](https://www.robotstxt.org/). The standard is voluntary. As the Wikipedia article on [web crawlers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_crawler) notes, "spambots and other malicious web crawlers are unlikely to place identifying information in the user agent field, or they may mask their identity as a browser or other well-known crawler."

Cloudflare found in July 2024 that bot operators attempt to appear as real browsers by using spoofed user agents. The company's global machine learning model scored spoofed traffic as likely bot regardless of the user agent string. A specific bot, identified by other researchers as Perplexity AI, had been observed to hide its activity behind browser-like user agents while operating from rotating IP addresses across different autonomous systems.

Unlike Perplexity, OpenAI's ChatGPT-User [fetched robots.txt and complied with crawl restrictions](https://blog.cloudflare.com/declaring-your-aindependence-block-ai-bots-scrapers-and-crawlers-with-a-single-click/), demonstrating that voluntary compliance is technically possible when operating in good faith. Cloudflare de-listed PerplexityBot from its verified bot directory following the disclosure.

## The dual-purpose Googlebot problem

[Cloudflare's September 2025 AI bot principles blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/building-a-better-internet-with-responsible-ai-bot-principles/) identified a structural problem. Googlebot crawls for both search indexing and AI training. The two purposes are combined in one crawler. A website that wants to appear in Google Search must allow Googlebot, which also means allowing the same crawl to feed Google's AI models. Google-Extended, the opt-out token for Gemini training, applies only to training and explicitly does not affect AI Overviews, which are attached to search.

OpenAI, by contrast, separates its crawlers. OAI-SearchBot only crawls for search. GPTBot only crawls for training. A publisher can allow one and block the other. The Cloudflare post argued that single-purpose crawlers are the responsible design and that combining purposes "forces an impossible choice onto website owners."

## AI crawlers by volume and reach

In July 2024, Cloudflare [published](https://blog.cloudflare.com/declaring-your-aindependence-block-ai-bots-scrapers-and-crawlers-with-a-single-click/) a network-wide survey of AI crawler traffic. The top four crawlers by request volume were Bytespider, operated by ByteDance; Amazonbot, reportedly used to index content for Alexa; ClaudeBot, Anthropic's crawler used to train Claude; and GPTBot, OpenAI's training crawler.

Bytespider accessed 40.40 percent of websites protected by Cloudflare. GPTBot accessed 35.46 percent. ClaudeBot accessed 11.17 percent. Among the top one million internet properties using Cloudflare, 38.73 percent were accessed by AI bots. Only 2.98 percent of those properties took action to block or challenge those requests. Among the top 10 sites by traffic, 80 percent were accessed and 40 percent blocked them.

The 2025 data showed changes. [The Year in Review](https://blog.cloudflare.com/radar-2025-year-in-review/) reported that Googlebot's crawl volume dwarfed other AI bots. Anthropic's ClaudeBot crawling doubled through the first half of 2025 before declining. PerplexityBot grew 3.5x over the year. Bytespider continued a decline that began in 2024, falling below several other training bots in volume.

AI user action crawling, where a chatbot visits a web page in response to a user question rather than for training, increased by over 15x in 2025. This is the fastest-growing category and represents a different kind of agentic traffic: a human asks a question, the AI visits a page to answer it, and the human consumes the answer without ever seeing the original site.

## The crawl-to-refer gap

In July 2025, [Cloudflare published](https://blog.cloudflare.com/ai-search-crawl-refer-ratio-on-radar/) a new metric: the crawl-to-refer ratio. It compares the number of pages an AI platform crawls to the number of visitors it refers back. The metric is designed to answer a simple question for publishers: for every page the AI reads from my site, how many visitors does it send me.

For the week of 19 to 26 June 2025, [Anthropic's ratio was 70,900 to 1](https://blog.cloudflare.com/ai-search-crawl-refer-ratio-on-radar/). For every 70,900 HTML pages Anthropic's crawlers requested, Claude referred one visitor back to a source website. Claude's native app does not include a referrer header, so the ratio likely overstates the gap, but the order of magnitude is consistent across platforms.

[OpenAI's ratio was approximately 3,700 to 1 at its peak](https://blog.cloudflare.com/radar-2025-year-in-review/) in March 2025, and fell as ChatGPT search functionality gained usage. [Perplexity had the lowest ratio among AI platforms, generally staying below 400 to 1](https://blog.cloudflare.com/radar-2025-year-in-review/), and below 200 to 1 from September 2025 onwards.

By comparison, traditional search engines had ratios in the single digits to low tens. Google's ratio was approximately 3 to 1 early in 2025, rising to 30 to 1 by mid-year before falling back. DuckDuckGo stayed below 1 to 1 for most of the year. Bing showed a cyclical weekly pattern between 50 and 70 to 1.

The business model of the web since the 1990s has been a trade: search engines crawl content and send traffic. The AI crawler model breaks that trade. The AI reads content and sends almost nobody back. [Cloudflare's CEO wrote on 1 July 2025](https://blog.cloudflare.com/content-independence-day-no-ai-crawl-without-compensation/) that with OpenAI it is 750 times harder to get traffic than it was with Google in the previous decade, and with Anthropic it is 30,000 times harder. That post declared "Content Independence Day" and announced that Cloudflare would change the default to block AI crawlers unless they pay creators.

## What publishers see

[Pew Research Center published a study in July 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/) analysing 68,879 Google searches from 900 US adults who shared their browsing data. Google users who encountered an AI-generated summary clicked on a traditional search result link in 8 percent of visits. Those who did not see a summary clicked nearly twice as often, 15 percent of visits. Users clicked on a link within the AI summary itself in only 1 percent of visits.

The same study found that 18 percent of Google searches produced an AI summary in March 2025. Wikipedia, YouTube, and Reddit accounted for 15 percent of sources cited in AI summaries. Government websites were cited more often in AI summaries, at 6 percent of sources, than in standard search results, at 2 percent.

Google's zero-click search rate on mobile had reached 75 percent before AI Overviews launched, meaning three-quarters of mobile searches were answered without the user leaving Google. AI Overviews, which generate summaries at the top of search pages, extended this pattern: Google was already sending less traffic, and AI summarisation sent even less.

In March 2024, Google reported that its search results were being inundated with websites that "feel like they were created for search engines instead of people." A Google spokesperson [told Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/google-search-ranking-spam-updates-2024-1839305119) that generative AI was responsible for the rapid proliferation of such content and that it could displace more valuable human-made alternatives. Google's [own blog post](https://blog.google/products/search/google-search-update-march-2024-core/) on the update said it would reduce "low-quality, unoriginal content" in search results by up to 40 percent.

## The protocols and the response

In September 2022, the Internet Engineering Task Force published [RFC 9309](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9309), the Robots Exclusion Protocol, formalising the robots.txt standard. The RFC explicitly states that robots.txt rules "are not a form of access authorization." The protocol is a request, not a gate.

In April 2026, the IETF AI Preferences Working Group published [draft-ietf-aipref-vocab-06](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-aipref-vocab/), defining a vocabulary for AI usage preferences. The vocabulary defines two core categories: Train-AI, for model training, and Search, for indexed content that directs users to the source. The draft, still in development and explicitly marked as not reflecting working group consensus, envisions a machine-readable preference layer more granular than robots.txt alone can provide.

Cloudflare introduced one-click AI crawler blocking in July 2024, AI Crawl Control analytics in [September 2024](https://blog.cloudflare.com/cloudflare-ai-audit-control-ai-content-crawlers/), and a Pay Per Crawl marketplace in July 2025. The Pay Per Crawl system uses Cloudflare's existing verified bot framework and enables publishers to set prices for AI crawler access by content type. The payment is priced per thousand tokens.

[The Internet Archive](https://archive.org/about/), which has crawled and [preserved the web since 1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Archive), provides a useful contrast. Its crawler obeys robots.txt and operates with a well-known user agent. Internet Archive crawlers are not training AI models. The organisation maintains an explicit line between preservation and commercial exploitation, though its crawlers consume the same server resources as AI training crawlers.

## Agent browsers

Traditional crawlers fetch HTML and parse text. A newer category, agent browsers, navigates websites visually. Anthropic announced [Claude computer use](https://docs.anthropic.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/computer-use) in October 2024, enabling Claude to view screens, move cursors, click buttons, and type text. Google followed with [Project Mariner](https://deepmind.google/technologies/project-mariner/) in December 2024, a Chrome extension that controls the browser to complete web tasks. The open-source project [Browser Use](https://github.com/browser-use/browser-use) provides a Python framework for AI agents to navigate websites programmatically.

Agent browsers consume far more resources than text crawlers. A text-only fetch of a web page may require 2 to 3 HTTP requests. A browser-based agent loads every image, every JavaScript bundle, every tracking pixel, and then generates a screenshot that must be processed by a vision model. A single agent browsing session can generate hundreds of requests to CDNs, analytics services, and ad networks. The traffic from one agent browser visit may equal a hundred traditional crawler requests.

The target is also different. Traditional crawlers index content for retrieval. Agent browsers consume content to perform actions: book a flight, file a support ticket, check a price. The website serves as infrastructure for the agent's task. No human visits the site. The business that operates the site receives no customer, no brand impression, and increasingly, no payment for the compute it incurs.

## The economic calculus

A website that serves a billion pages per month to humans generates revenue from the humans who arrive: subscriptions, ad impressions, purchases. A website that serves a billion pages per month to AI crawlers generates infrastructure costs.

The Cloudflare Pay Per Crawl marketplace, announced in July 2025, is one attempt to build a settlement layer. Publishers set a price per thousand tokens for AI crawler access. The price is charged per content type: news, research, reference, and so on. The marketplace uses Cloudflare's verified bot framework to authenticate crawler identity. Whether it succeeds depends on adoption, which in turn depends on whether the AI companies paying for access find it cheaper than the legal risk of crawling without payment.

The larger problem is structural. The web's funding model was built on the assumption that content and audience were connected through the same infrastructure. AI uncouples them. A publisher produces content. An AI model consumes it. A human reader sees a summary. The publisher pays for the compute to serve the AI, and again for the compute to serve the human, but the AI-to-human step generates no revenue for the publisher. The reader may never learn the name of the publication that created the underlying story.

[The New York Times sued OpenAI and Microsoft in December 2023](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/12/27/business/media/new-york-times-open-ai-microsoft-lawsuit.html) alleging copyright infringement through the use of its articles for training. The case is ongoing. Multiple other publishers, including the Chicago Tribune, Denver Post, and Alden Global Capital publications, have filed similar suits. The outcome of these cases will determine whether the legal system extends copyright protection to the ingestion layer, or treats AI training as a novel category outside existing frameworks.

Google's [AI Overviews](https://blog.google/products/search/generative-ai-search/) launched in May 2024, generating summaries at the top of search results pages. The company reported that it includes links to source sites within the summaries, and that AI Overviews send more traffic to publishers than traditional search listings for the same query. This claim has been disputed by publishers who point to aggregate declines in organic search traffic since the feature launched.

## Common Crawl and the open data question

[Common Crawl](https://commoncrawl.org/faq), the non-profit that maintains a free, open repository of web crawl data used extensively for AI training, identifies itself as CCBot. Its FAQ states that the crawler obeys robots.txt and supports crawl-delay directives. The foundation published [dedicated IP ranges](https://commoncrawl.org/ccbot) for verification in November 2024 and supports reverse DNS lookups for bot verification.

The organisation released its [July 2026 crawl archive](https://commoncrawl.org/blog/july-2026-crawl-archive-now-available) containing 2.14 billion web pages, or 364 TiB of uncompressed content, crawled between 7 and 25 July 2026. Its [web graph for May to July 2026](https://commoncrawl.org/blog/host--and-domain-level-web-graphs-may-june-and-july-2026) contains 240.4 million nodes and 3.7 billion edges at the host level. The foundation also published an [AI Visibility Audit](https://commoncrawl.org/blog/introducing-the-ai-visibility-audit), a guide for publishers to check whether AI systems can reach their sites and how to stay visible in the crawl that trains them.

The question Common Crawl raises is fundamental. A non-profit runs a crawler that obeys robots.txt and publishes its data openly. The same data is used by commercial AI companies to train models that crawl 70,900 pages for every one they refer. The crawler is the same mechanism. The downstream effects are not.

## The history of bots on the open web

Robots have been part of the web since its earliest days. The robotstxt.org resource, maintained since 1994, defines web robots as "programs that traverse the Web automatically." The [Robots Exclusion Protocol](https://www.robotstxt.org/robotstxt.html) was proposed by Martijn Koster in 1994 after his server was overwhelmed by a poorly written crawler. The protocol has been a voluntary standard ever since.

In 2017, [The Atlantic published](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2017/01/bots-bots-bots/515043/) an article titled "The Internet Is Mostly Bots," reporting on Imperva's 2016 data that 52 percent of web traffic was automated. In 2018, [New York Magazine ran](https://nymag.com/intelligencer/2018/12/how-much-of-the-internet-is-fake.html) "How Much of the Internet Is Fake? Turns Out, a Lot of It, Actually," cataloguing fake views on YouTube, fake followers on social media, and programmatic ad fraud. These articles predate the AI training era. The bots they describe were selling fake engagement. The bots of 2026 are consuming real content to train models that replace engagement entirely.

## What August 2026 shows

The current data from Cloudflare Radar, covering the week to 12 August 2026, shows a 35.6 percent bot share of HTTP requests. The United States originated 42.6 percent of those bot requests. Microsoft, AWS, and Google cloud infrastructure originated approximately 32 percent of global bot traffic between them.

The most recent comprehensive AI crawler data remains the 2025 Year in Review, which found AI bots at 4.2 percent of HTML requests and Googlebot at 4.5 percent, user action crawling up 15x, and AI crawlers as the most frequently disallowed user agents in robots.txt files. The crawl-to-refer gap, with Anthropic at 70,900 to 1 and OpenAI at 3,700 to 1 at peak, is the metric that explains the publisher response.

The dead internet theory, for years treated as a fringe conspiracy, has proven to be the conceptual framework that most closely maps onto the observable shift. In its leaner form, stripped of the conspiracy elements, the theory simply states that automated content is displacing human-authored content and that the economic and social structures built for human-to-human connection were not designed for this transition. The core observation is not that bots outnumber people. It is that the protocols and business models of the open web never anticipated a world where machines consume content at industrial scale and refer almost nothing back.
