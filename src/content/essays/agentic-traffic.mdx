## The current picture

In the seven days to 12 August 2026, 35.6 percent of HTTP requests on Cloudflare's global network came from bots, not humans. The remaining 64.4 percent was human traffic. The figure is from [Cloudflare Radar](https://radar.cloudflare.com/bots), which processes data from Cloudflare's network of 330 cities serving an average of 81 million HTTP requests per second.

By location, the United States originated 42.6 percent of all bot traffic. Germany was second at 5.9 percent, the Netherlands third at 4.7 percent. By autonomous system, Microsoft originated 9 percent, Amazon Web Services 14.7 percent across two AS numbers, and Google 8.5 percent. Three cloud providers generate nearly a third of the world's bot traffic, and the bulk of AI crawler traffic runs on their infrastructure.

The 35.6 percent figure is lower than the widely cited statistic that bots account for more than half of all web traffic. That number comes from Imperva's 2016 Bad Bot Report, published nearly a decade ago, and has been repeated in Wikipedia and security marketing materials ever since. The current share on Cloudflare's network is smaller. Cloudflare mitigates approximately 6 percent of global traffic as potentially malicious, and its free-tier AI crawler blocking feature, launched in July 2024, has been adopted by a large share of its customer base. The combination of mitigation and blocking reduces the observed bot share relative to raw internet traffic.

The composition of the 35.6 percent matters more than the total. Most bot traffic is not AI crawlers. The [Cloudflare 2025 Year in Review](https://blog.cloudflare.com/radar-2025-year-in-review/), covering January through December 2025, found that search engine crawlers generated 40 percent of verified bot traffic, AI crawlers 20 percent, and search engine optimization bots 13 percent. Googlebot alone, which crawls for both search indexing and AI training, originated more than a quarter of all verified bot traffic and 4.5 percent of HTML requests — more than all other AI bots combined at 4.2 percent.

## The AI crawler landscape

In July 2024, Cloudflare [published](https://blog.cloudflare.com/declaring-your-aindependence-block-ai-bots-scrapers-and-crawlers-with-a-single-click/) a network-wide survey of AI crawler traffic. The top four crawlers by request volume were Bytespider, operated by ByteDance, the Chinese owner of TikTok; Amazonbot, reportedly used to index content for Alexa; ClaudeBot, Anthropic's crawler used to train Claude; and GPTBot, OpenAI's training crawler.

Bytespider accessed 40.40 percent of websites protected by Cloudflare. GPTBot accessed 35.46 percent. ClaudeBot accessed 11.17 percent. Among the top one million internet properties using Cloudflare, 38.73 percent were accessed by AI bots. Only 2.98 percent of those properties took action to block or challenge those requests. Among the top 10 sites by traffic, 80 percent were accessed and 40 percent blocked them.

The 2025 data showed changes. [Cloudflare's 2025 Year in Review](https://blog.cloudflare.com/radar-2025-year-in-review/) reported that Googlebot's crawl volume dwarfed other AI bots. Anthropic's ClaudeBot crawling doubled through the first half of 2025 before declining. PerplexityBot grew 3.5x over the year. Bytespider continued a decline that began in 2024, falling below several other training bots in volume.

AI user action crawling, where a chatbot visits a web page in response to a user question rather than for training, increased by over 15x in 2025. This is the fastest-growing category and represents a different kind of agentic traffic: a human asks a question, the AI visits a page to answer it, and the human consumes the answer without ever seeing the original site.

## The crawl-to-refer gap

In July 2025, [Cloudflare published](https://blog.cloudflare.com/ai-search-crawl-refer-ratio-on-radar/) a new metric: the crawl-to-refer ratio. It compares the number of pages an AI platform crawls to the number of visitors it refers back. The metric is designed to answer a simple question for publishers: for every page the AI reads from my site, how many visitors does it send me.

For the week of 19-26 June 2025, [Anthropic's ratio was 70,900 to 1](https://blog.cloudflare.com/ai-search-crawl-refer-ratio-on-radar/). For every 70,900 HTML pages Anthropic's crawlers requested, Claude referred one visitor back to a source website. The company noted that Claude's native app does not include a referrer header, so the ratio likely overstates the gap, but the order of magnitude is consistent across platforms.

[OpenAI's ratio was approximately 3,700 to 1 at its peak](https://blog.cloudflare.com/radar-2025-year-in-review/) in March 2025, and fell as ChatGPT search functionality, which includes links to source sites, gained usage. [Perplexity had the lowest ratio among AI platforms, generally staying below 400 to 1](https://blog.cloudflare.com/radar-2025-year-in-review/), and below 200 to 1 from September 2025 onwards.

By comparison, traditional search engines had ratios in the single digits to low tens. Google's ratio was approximately 3 to 1 early in 2025, rising to 30 to 1 by mid-year before falling back. DuckDuckGo stayed below 1 to 1 for most of the year. Microsoft's Bing showed a cyclical weekly pattern between 50 and 70 to 1.

The business model of the web since the 1990s has been: search engines crawl content and send traffic. The AI crawler model breaks that trade. The AI reads content and sends almost nobody back. [Cloudflare's CEO wrote on 1 July 2025](https://blog.cloudflare.com/content-independence-day-no-ai-crawl-without-compensation/) that with OpenAI it is 750 times more difficult to get traffic than it was with Google in the previous decade, and with Anthropic it is 30,000 times more difficult. That post declared "Content Independence Day" and announced that Cloudflare would change the default to block AI crawlers unless they pay creators.

## What publishers see

[Pew Research Center published a study in July 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/) analyzing 68,879 Google searches from 900 US adults who shared their browsing data. The finding: Google users who encountered an AI-generated summary clicked on a traditional search result link in 8 percent of visits. Those who did not see a summary clicked nearly twice as often, 15 percent of visits. Users clicked on a link within the AI summary itself in only 1 percent of visits.

The same study found that 18 percent of Google searches produced an AI summary in March 2025. Wikipedia, YouTube, and Reddit accounted for 15 percent of sources cited in AI summaries. Government websites were cited more often in AI summaries (6 percent of sources) than in standard search results (2 percent).

The direct effect on publishers is measurable with Cloudflare's own data. [Google's zero-click search rate on mobile](https://blog.cloudflare.com/content-independence-day-no-ai-crawl-without-compensation/) had reached 75 percent before AI Overviews launched, meaning three-quarters of mobile searches were answered without the user leaving Google. AI Overviews, which generate summaries at the top of search pages, extended this pattern: Google was already sending less traffic, and AI summarisation sent even less.

## How crawlers identify themselves, and how they deceive

Every major AI company publishes documentation for its crawlers. [OpenAI lists four](https://platform.openai.com/docs/bots): GPTBot for training, OAI-SearchBot for search, ChatGPT-User for user-driven visits, and OAI-AdsBot for ad verification. [Google publishes](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawlers-fetchers/google-common-crawlers) Googlebot for search and AI training combined, plus Google-Extended as an opt-out token for Gemini training and grounding. [Perplexity lists](https://docs.perplexity.ai/guides/bots) PerplexityBot for search and Perplexity-User for user-driven visits. Anthropic's crawler documentation is behind a support page.

Cloudflare found in July 2024 that bot operators attempt to appear as real browsers by using spoofed user agents. The company's global machine learning model scored spoofed traffic as likely bot regardless of the user agent string. [A specific bot, identified by other researchers as Perplexity](https://blog.cloudflare.com/declaring-your-aindependence-block-ai-bots-scrapers-and-crawlers-with-a-single-click/), had been observed to hide its activity behind browser-like user agents.

## The dual-purpose Googlebot problem

[Cloudflare's September 2025 AI bot principles blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/building-a-better-internet-with-responsible-ai-bot-principles/) identified a specific structural problem. Googlebot crawls for both search indexing and AI training. The two purposes are combined in one crawler. A website that wants to appear in Google Search must allow Googlebot, which also means allowing the same crawl to feed Google's AI models. Google-Extended, the opt-out token for Gemini training, applies only to training and explicitly does not affect AI Overviews, which are attached to search.

OpenAI, by contrast, separates its crawlers. OAI-SearchBot only crawls for search. GPTBot only crawls for training. A publisher can allow one and block the other. The Cloudflare post argued that single-purpose crawlers are the responsible design, and that combining purposes "forces an impossible choice onto website owners."

## The policy response

In September 2022, the Internet Engineering Task Force published [RFC 9309](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9309), the Robots Exclusion Protocol, formalising the robots.txt standard that had operated informally since 1994. The RFC explicitly states that robots.txt rules "are not a form of access authorization." The protocol is a request, not a gate.

In April 2026, the IETF AI Preferences Working Group published [draft-ietf-aipref-vocab-06](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-aipref-vocab/), defining a vocabulary for AI usage preferences. The vocabulary defines two core categories: train-ai, for model training, and search, for indexed content that directs users to the source. The draft, still in development and explicitly marked as not reflecting working group consensus, envisions a machine-readable preference layer more granular than robots.txt alone can provide.

Cloudflare introduced [one-click AI crawler blocking in July 2024](https://blog.cloudflare.com/declaring-your-aindependence-block-ai-bots-scrapers-and-crawlers-with-a-single-click/), [AI Crawl Control analytics in September 2024](https://blog.cloudflare.com/cloudflare-ai-audit-control-ai-content-crawlers/), and a [Pay Per Crawl marketplace in July 2025](https://blog.cloudflare.com/content-independence-day-no-ai-crawl-without-compensation/). The company published a new bot taxonomy in [July 2026](https://developers.cloudflare.com/bots/concepts/bot/verified-bots/) that classifies bots by behaviour, Search, Agent, and Training, rather than by legacy categories. It also introduced [Web Bot Auth](https://developers.cloudflare.com/bots/reference/bot-verification/web-bot-auth/), a cryptographic verification mechanism for bot identity, replacing user-agent-based trust with cryptographic attestation.

## Common Crawl and the open data question

[Common Crawl](https://commoncrawl.org/faq), the non-profit that maintains a free, open repository of web crawl data used extensively for AI training, identifies itself as CCBot. Its FAQ states that the crawler obeys robots.txt and supports crawl-delay directives. Common Crawl published dedicated IP ranges for verification in November 2024 and supports reverse DNS lookups for bot verification.

The organisation runs its crawler on dedicated infrastructure with an adaptive back-off algorithm designed to prevent server overload. Its FAQ offers standard robots.txt blocking instructions for site owners who want to opt out. The organisation also maintains an opt-out registry.

## What August 2026 shows

The current data from Cloudflare Radar, covering the week to 12 August 2026, shows a 35.6 percent bot share of HTTP requests. The United States originated 42.6 percent of those bot requests. Microsoft, AWS, and Google cloud infrastructure originated approximately 32 percent of global bot traffic between them.

The most recent comprehensive AI crawler data remains the 2025 Year in Review, which found AI bots at 4.2 percent of HTML requests and Googlebot at 4.5 percent, user action crawling up 15x, and AI crawlers as the most frequently disallowed user agents in robots.txt files. The crawl-to-refer gap, with Anthropic at 70,900 to 1 and OpenAI at 3,700 to 1 at peak, is the metric that explains the publisher response.

One year after Content Independence Day, [Cloudflare's July 2026 retrospective](https://blog.cloudflare.com/,) described a dynamic market for monetized content and detailed the infrastructure required for a sustainable web economy in the age of autonomous AI agents. The specific crawl-to-refer numbers for mid-2026 are not yet public in the same detail as the 2025 data, and this piece will be updated when the 2026 Year in Review publishes.
