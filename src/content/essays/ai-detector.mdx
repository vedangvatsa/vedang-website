By early 2024, telling human writing from AI text was getting messy. Existing detectors broke the moment you paraphrased the text or ran it through a new model. I wanted to build a fast, readable detector that could run at scale without hiding its limitations. Here is the engineering process.

## The training corpus

The detector depends entirely on the corpus. I built a dataset of 2.77 million texts across academic writing, news, social media, and creative writing, split into 1.85 million AI-generated samples and 920,000 human samples. Stylistic signals differ wildly by domain. Academic writing uses formal connectors, while social media relies on short, conversational sentences with frequent self-mentions. Training one general model on everything confused style differences with the AI-vs-human signal. I ended up training separate models for each domain, plus a general fallback model.

## Why stylometry?

Instead of relying on heavy, slow, and expensive transformers like RoBERTa or GPTZero that overfit to specific models, I focused on stylometry. I extracted interpretable metrics (sentence length variance, vocabulary diversity, repetition, punctuation entropy) and trained a Random Forest. This approach is fast, runs cheaply on standard CPUs, and shows exactly which features triggered the classification.

## The eleven core features

After testing dozens of features, I kept eleven for production, including vocabulary diversity (MTLD), sentence length variance, self-mention density, sentence openers, connector density, hedges, and punctuation entropy. These metrics capture a common trait of AI text: it is highly uniform, predictable, and balanced. Human writing is noisier, with irregular sentence lengths and random repetitions.

## Expanding to thirty-five features

I experimented with adding twenty-four more features covering readability indexes, sentiment analysis, syntax trees, and named-entity density. This expansion raised the model's AUC from 0.9645 to 0.9826 and accuracy from 0.9011 to 0.9361. Despite the metrics boost, I stuck with the eleven-feature set for the production API. The performance overhead and complexity of the extra features weren't worth the small gain in real-world deployment.

## Benchmark performance

Public benchmarks showed the limits of single-domain models. While within-register performance was strong (AUCs between 0.933 and 0.978), testing across different domains dropped the mean AUC to 0.728. 

Adversarial robustness was solid. Simple edits like swapping synonyms or tweaking sentence lengths only lowered the AUC by 0.009. More aggressive paraphrasing on the RAID benchmark dropped the score to 0.951, proving that heavy re-writing can still bypass stylometric detection.

## Multi-model pipelines

To handle edge cases, I integrated public transformers from HuggingFace, including RoBERTa-large and the MAGE Longformer. Because no single model succeeded across all types of text, I routed inputs through specialized pipelines. The final local runs hit 0.9997 on the HC3 dataset and 0.9146 on TuringBench.

## Fine-tuning on TuringBench

I fine-tuned RoBERTa-large on the TuringBench dataset (331,000 texts) using Kaggle. After a single training epoch, the validation AUC reached 0.9991. Fine-tuned transformers are highly effective when you have enough niche data, but stylometry remains the better, cheaper fallback when running zero-shot or out-of-domain.

## API engineering

Moving from a notebook to production required hardening the API. I built FastAPI endpoints, added domain classification, integrated rate limiting, and set up in-memory caching. I also added text preprocessing to strip basic adversarial humanizers and calibrated scores for short inputs.

## Auditing the repo

Once the API was stable, I ran an audit to clean up the code. I aligned the README benchmarks with actual results, removed duplicate batch endpoints, and consolidated the feature extraction logic. I also extracted duplicate security checks into a shared module and cleaned up unused dependencies to make the project reproducible.

## Key takeaways

Interpretability doesn't destroy performance. A simple Random Forest with carefully chosen features can compete with deep neural networks on structure. Furthermore, domain alignment matters more than parameter size, and ensembles out-perform single models. In production, caching, rate limiting, and calibration matter as much as model accuracy.

<KeyTakeaway>
The best detector is not the one with the highest benchmark score. It is the one you can explain, deploy cheaply, and trust under the attack patterns your users actually use.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Limitations

Stylometry fails against aggressive paraphrasing and translation loops. It requires longer text inputs to remain reliable, and cross-domain classification drops to 0.728 AUC.

## Next steps

I plan to integrate a part-of-speech pipeline, add a per-sentence heatmap for visual text analysis, and optimize performance for low false-positive environments.
