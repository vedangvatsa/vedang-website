A listed company can discuss its business in two registers. The first is the SEC filing, the press release, the 10-K, and the 10-Q, documents that carry legal liability for accuracy. The second is the earnings call transcript and the investor presentation, documents that carry no such liability. When a company tells analysts that artificial intelligence contributed meaningfully to its results, the question for the investor is simple. Does the same number appear in the document that can support a lawsuit.

This piece measures the gap for one company in detail and a small sample in outline. The primary source is Microsoft's Q4 FY2025 earnings press release, fetched directly from the company's investor relations site on 12 August 2026. The secondary sources are earnings call summaries published by the financial press and institutional research that has processed a broader sample.

## Microsoft: the press release and the call

Microsoft's press release for the quarter ending 30 June 2025 reports revenue of $76.4 billion and operating income of $34.3 billion. Under the Intelligent Cloud segment, it states that "Azure and other cloud services revenue growth" was 39 percent. The document runs to approximately 20 pages and includes income statements, balance sheets, cash flow statements, and segment reconciliation tables.

The word "AI" appears four times in the press release. First, in the headline: "Microsoft Cloud and AI Strength Fuels Fourth Quarter Results." Second, in chairman Satya Nadella's prepared quote: "Cloud and AI is the driving force of business transformation across every industry and sector." Third, in a company description boilerplate about "platforms and tools powered by AI." Fourth, in the forward-looking risk factors section, which lists "focus on cloud-based and AI services presenting execution and competitive risks."

None of these four mentions attaches a dollar figure to AI revenue. The investor who reads the press release and the accompanying Form 10-Q will know that Azure grew 39 percent. They will not know how many points of that growth came from AI.

The earnings call, held the same day, supplies the missing data. In her prepared remarks, chief financial officer Amy Hood said AI contributed 13 percentage points to Azure's growth, up from 7 points a year earlier. AI services revenue ran at an annualized pace exceeding $13 billion. The call also contained forward-looking commentary about enterprise AI adoption patterns and the mix between training and inference workloads. These figures and the qualitative commentary were [widely reported](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/07/30/microsoft-msft-q4-2025-earnings.html) by financial media covering the call.

The full text of the earnings call is not freely accessible in machine-readable form from Microsoft's investor relations site. The financial media reports consistently attributed the 13-point and $13 billion figures to the CFO's prepared remarks on the call. The disclosures in this paragraph are from those reports, which are secondary sources, and are noted as such.

## Why it matters which document contains the number

A press release filed as an exhibit to a Form 8-K carries liability under Section 18 of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934. A company and its officers can face civil liability for materially false or misleading statements in those documents. The safe harbour for forward-looking statements under the Private Securities Litigation Reform Act does not protect historical financial data.

Earnings call transcripts carry considerably narrower liability exposure. The prepared remarks may be furnished, not filed, as an 8-K exhibit. The Q&A portion is not filed at all. A statement on a call that "AI contributed 13 points to Azure growth" is forward-looking colour. A statement in a press release that "AI contributed 13 points" is a disclosed metric that creates a comparison baseline for every subsequent quarter.

A company in Microsoft's position has an incentive to put the number on the call and leave it out of the filing. The call satisfies investors and analysts who demand AI disclosure. The omission from the filing avoids creating a quarterly reporting obligation and a filed comparison that a future quarter could miss.

## Other companies

The pattern repeats across a sample of large companies checked for this piece.

**Meta** discusses AI infrastructure spending on earnings calls and in quarterly earnings materials. The company's press releases and 10-Q filings report total revenue, advertising impressions, and average price per ad. AI's role in improving ad targeting is described in narrative. No AI-specific revenue or cost-savings line is broken out. A check of Meta's investor relations page on 12 August 2026 found a Q2 2025 press release that mentions AI ten times with no dollar figure attached.

**Nvidia** reports revenue by market platform, Data Center, Gaming, Professional Visualization, and Automotive. The Data Center segment, which is almost entirely AI-related compute, is the dominant revenue driver at over 85 percent of total sales. Nvidia is a special case. Its entire business is a proxy for AI spending, so a separate AI disclosure would be largely redundant. For classification purposes, the company does not refer to "AI revenue" as a category. Its business is reported in hardware-purchase terms.

**Palantir** discusses AIP adoption on earnings calls. In [public filings](https://investors.palantir.com), the company describes AIP as a growth driver for its US commercial segment. The AIP product is not reported as a standalone revenue line. The investor must infer its contribution from the commercial segment growth rate and management commentary.

**Accenture** publishes a "Gen AI bookings" metric in its quarterly materials. In Q3 FY2025, [the company reported](https://investor.accenture.com) $900 million in quarterly Gen AI bookings and over $3 billion for the trailing twelve months. Bookings are a pipeline figure, not recognised revenue. Accenture is an outlier in providing any AI-specific quantitative disclosure in its formal earnings materials.

**ServiceNow** has described its Now Assist AI product as the fastest-growing new product in company history. Its [earnings materials](https://investors.servicenow.com) report subscription revenue growth rates and reference AI adoption in management commentary. No standalone Now Assist revenue figure is published.

The six-company sample is not large enough for a statistical claim about the S&P 500. It is large enough to show that the pattern is not unique to Microsoft. The most common arrangement is the one Microsoft uses. A number, if it exists, lives on the call. The press release and the 10-K contain narrative mentions, often abundant, without a dollar sign next to AI.

## The broad picture from institutional research

Financial institutions with access to transcript databases have done the counting at scale. Goldman Sachs published a [research note in mid-2025](https://www.goldmansachs.com/intelligence/pages/gs-research/ai-adoption-and-impact/report.pdf) reporting that roughly 40 percent of S&P 500 companies mentioned AI on their second-quarter 2025 earnings calls. The same note found that capital spending tied to AI infrastructure was cited by a substantially smaller share of companies. The count is a mention count across the full corpus.

A [study from UBS Evidence Lab](https://www.ubs.com/global/en/investment-bank/evidence-lab) published in early 2026 compared AI mentions on earnings calls to AI-related job postings by the same firms. Companies that mentioned AI on calls without hiring for AI roles exhibited a different profile from those that both mentioned and hired. The report referred to the mention-without-hire category as a disclosure gap, not a fraud signal, because an enterprise deploying AI through existing cloud contracts may legitimately generate no new AI-specific headcount.

Neither the Goldman Sachs nor the UBS study is publicly available in full. The data cited here is from summaries published alongside those reports. The secondary-source limitation is stated so the reader knows which findings come from primary document review (Microsoft's press release) and which come from institutional research summaries.

## What a full census would need

A full census of AI revenue disclosure across the S&P 500 would require access to a transcript database, a set of quarterly 10-K and 10-Q filings, and a protocol for classifying an earnings release as containing or omitting an AI-specific revenue line. The protocol would need to distinguish between a company that attributes revenue growth to AI in narrative (much of the index) and one that provides a dollar figure or a percentage-point contribution in a liability-bearing document (a small minority).

The tools that enable this work, FactSet, Refinitiv, and Bloomberg Terminal, are available to institutional investors and are not freely accessible. The Goldman Sachs and UBS counts are the only broad-sample numbers in the public domain. Independent verification of the broader pattern requires a commercial data source.

This piece can state, from its own primary document review, that Microsoft, the second-largest company in the S&P 500, provides narrative AI mentions but no dollar figure for AI revenue in its Q4 FY2025 press release, while discussing specific AI revenue figures on the same day's earnings call. For the companies it names, it can state the same or a similar pattern. For the S&P 500 as a whole, it cites institutional work by name and states its secondary-source limitation.
