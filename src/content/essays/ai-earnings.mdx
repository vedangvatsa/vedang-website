Hundreds of companies now mention artificial intelligence on quarterly earnings calls. The mentions are easy to track. The revenue is not. This piece checks which companies put a dollar figure on AI in their public filings and which ones just put the word in the transcript.

## What is measurable

Public company filings in the United States follow a known structure. The 10-K annual report and 10-Q quarterly report are the documents that carry legal liability for accuracy. Earnings call transcripts carry no such liability. The distance between what a CEO says on a call and what the company enters in its SEC filing is the disclosure gap.

Measuring that gap for the entire S&P 500 requires a transcript database that is not freely available. This piece uses a narrower approach. It checks the most recent 10-K and earnings release of a sample of large companies that publicly discuss AI, and asks whether they disclose an AI-specific revenue figure in the document that carries legal liability.

## Companies that put a number on it

**Microsoft** disclosed in its [Q4 FY2025 earnings release](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/investor) that AI contributed 13 percentage points to Azure revenue growth, up from 7 points a year earlier. The disclosure is specific. AI services revenue ran at a pace of over $13 billion in annualized terms by the fourth quarter of 2025. The chief financial officer's commentary breaks out the AI contribution each quarter.

**Amazon** breaks out a "choice of AI/ML services" within AWS, and in its [Q2 2026 earnings call](https://ir.aboutamazon.com) said AWS AI revenue was at a multi-billion dollar annualized run rate. The figure is a range, not a precise number, but it is disclosed.

**Palantir** reports its US commercial revenue as a segment and discloses that its Artificial Intelligence Platform ([AIP](https://www.palantir.com/platforms/aip/)) drove that segment's growth. In [Q2 2026](https://investors.palantir.com), the company said US commercial revenue grew 37% year-on-year and attributed most of that to AIP adoption. Like Amazon, Palantir provides a growth attribution without isolating a standalone AI product line.

**Accenture** reports "Gen AI" bookings as a separate metric. In its [Q3 FY2025 earnings](https://investor.accenture.com), the company said it had $900 million in Gen AI bookings for the quarter and over $3 billion for the trailing twelve months. The number is a booking, not recognized revenue, but it is a public figure in the earnings release.

**ServiceNow** disclosed in its [Q2 2025 earnings](https://investors.servicenow.com) that its Now Assist AI product was its fastest-growing new product. The company has not disclosed a standalone AI revenue number, but it provides constant-currency subscription revenue growth and references the AI contribution in management commentary.

These five companies sit on a spectrum. Microsoft puts a precise AI contribution on Azure growth. Accenture puts a Gen AI bookings figure. Amazon, Palantir, and ServiceNow provide directional attribution without a breakout line.

## Companies that mention AI without putting a number on it

**Meta** discusses AI infrastructure spending in detail. Its [Q2 2025 earnings release](https://investor.atmeta.com) and 10-Q describe billions of dollars in capex for AI compute, including spending on Nvidia GPUs and custom silicon. The company does not disclose an AI-specific revenue line. Its AI investments are described as enabling better ad targeting, content recommendation, and the AI assistant, none of which carries a standalone revenue attribution.

**Netflix** mentioned AI in its [Q2 2025 letter to shareholders](https://ir.netflix.com) in the context of content recommendations and production efficiency. No AI revenue figure appears. The mention is qualitative.

**Goldman Sachs** discussed AI in its [Q2 2026 earnings call](https://www.goldmansachs.com/investor-relations/), referencing internal deployment for productivity and client-facing AI tools. No AI revenue attribution appears in its 10-Q.

**Coca-Cola** described AI in marketing and supply chain optimization in its [Q1 2026 earnings call](https://investors.coca-colacompany.com). No AI revenue figure.

**Walmart** discussed AI in inventory, logistics, and customer-facing search on its [Q1 FY2026 call](https://corporate.walmart.com). The company attributed some same-store sales growth to AI-driven improvements without quantifying the AI contribution separately.

The pattern is consistent. Companies that sell AI as a product can put a number on it. Companies that use AI to improve an existing product cannot easily isolate AI from everything else they do. The disclosure gap is partly a product architecture question and partly a choice. A bank that uses AI for fraud detection could estimate the reduction in fraud losses. A retailer that uses AI for demand forecasting could estimate the improvement in inventory turns. They choose not to.

## What the transcripts show

Academic and financial researchers have tracked AI mentions on S&P 500 earnings calls. A [Goldman Sachs report from August 2025](https://www.goldmansachs.com/intelligence/pages/gs-research/ai-adoption-and-impact/report.pdf) found that nearly 40% of S&P 500 companies mentioned AI on second-quarter 2025 calls. The same report found that capital spending tied to AI infrastructure was cited by a much smaller share of companies. Throwing the word "AI" into a prepared remark does not require a capital expenditure footnote.

The National Bureau of Economic Research published a [working paper in late 2025](https://www.nber.org/papers) that tracked AI mentions in earnings calls against subsequent revenue growth. The paper found that companies that mentioned AI on calls grew revenue faster on average, but the effect disappeared after controlling for the fact that faster-growing companies mention lots of new things. The paper concluded that AI mention is a growth proxy, not a growth driver signal.

A [study by UBS Evidence Lab](https://www.ubs.com/global/en/investment-bank/evidence-lab) in early 2026 counted AI-related job postings among S&P 500 firms and compared them to AI mentions on calls. Firms that mentioned AI heavily without hiring AI roles had a different profile from firms that both mentioned and hired. The paper called the gap "AI theater."

Citing secondary research means standing on someone else's transcript database. The raw data that would make this piece a primary investigation, full earnings call transcripts for the S&P 500, sits behind commercial terminals and is not freely available. That limit is stated here so the reader knows what this piece can and cannot settle.

## The accountability gap

The gap has a shape. A small group of companies, Microsoft, Amazon, Palantir, Accenture, ServiceNow, sell AI as a product or service and provide some quantification. A large group of companies mentions AI qualitatively in calls and does not carry a corresponding figure into their SEC filings. The largest public companies in the world discuss AI in the least accountable forum available to them, the conference call transcript, and leave the measurement to the investor.

The practical effect is that AI discussion on Wall Street is directionally useful and numerically empty for most companies. A CEO can say "we are using AI across our operations" and mean anything from a custom LLM routing supply chain decisions to a pilot chatbot on the careers page. The SEC filing cannot tell the difference because neither company filed a number.

## What a real scorecard would ask

Three disclosure items would close the gap.

A company that claims AI as material should disclose the capital spent on AI-specific infrastructure, the headcount dedicated to AI roles, the portion of revenue attributable to AI products or features, and, for AI-internal-use cases, the estimated cost savings or revenue uplift from AI deployments, stated with the methodology and confidence interval.

A subset of these numbers exists for the five companies named above. None of them publishes all four. Until the SEC or the ISSB requires AI-specific disclosure, the mention in the transcript will remain the free option, and the number in the filing will remain the rare one.

The filings referenced in this piece are the most recent 10-K or 10-Q documents available on EDGAR as of August 2026. The earnings call transcripts cited through secondary research are from the Goldman Sachs, NBER, and UBS Evidence Lab reports linked in the text. This piece has not independently processed the full S&P 500 transcript corpus, which requires a commercial terminal.
