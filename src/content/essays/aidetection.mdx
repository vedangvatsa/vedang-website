I would be careful about using an AI detector to question someone's writing. In [my study](https://github.com/vedangvatsa/research-paper-framework/blob/076f6f45c7a71d801cb58d202938422b39b48e1f/papers/linguistic-markers-paper/linguistic_markers_paper.pdf), one detector setup scored 95.99% on one collection of research abstracts and 53.89% on another. Both tests used short summaries of research papers.

An accuracy score counts how often the detector agrees with the labels in a test. It does not tell us how every writer produced a text. Before using that number to judge someone, I would want to know what the test covered and what the detector got wrong.

## The result did not hold up

The first test used texts held back from training. But they still came from the same collection.

I then tested the approach on [a separately collected set of abstracts](https://huggingface.co/datasets/NicolaiSivesind/ChatGPT-Research-Abstracts/tree/07857e2a97571ab553421ed645ef470bef6fa05c). I checked for overlap and removed the matches I found. The detector did not use the remaining texts for training or for choosing its original cutoff.

On this second test, it caught 976 of the 8,319 abstracts labelled as AI-generated. It missed 7,343. It caught about 12 of every 100 generated examples.

It still labelled most of the published abstracts correctly. That is why the overall accuracy score does not show how often it missed the generated texts. I need to look at the two kinds of mistake separately: wrong flags and missed detections.

## Length was a useful clue

The main detector used eight text measurements. These included sentence lengths, word variety, and counts of words such as “may” or “however.” It had no access to the writer's notes or drafts.

The generated abstracts in the original collection clustered around 200 words. The published abstracts varied more in length. A version of the detector using only word and sentence counts reached about 90% accuracy on that collection.

On the separate collection, the same length-only approach scored about 52%.

Length helped separate the labels in the first set of texts. It worked poorly in the second. That is a reason to check whether a detector has found a useful clue for a new use, or a pattern tied to the data it learned from.

Length did not explain the whole result. When I trained and tested the full model on pairs where both texts had 150-250 words, it still scored 94.82%. There were other useful differences within that set. The harder question was whether they would hold up elsewhere.

I could not identify one cause for the gap between the collections. They differed in several ways, including the reported generator, prompts, and source mix. The data did not separate those effects.

## A wrong flag still needs an answer

The detector wrongly flagged 328 of the published abstracts in the second test. These were errors against the labels in the data.

A reviewer would still need evidence about the document before making a claim about its writer. The files also lacked a complete writing history for every text. Neither a detector score nor a label in a dataset can supply that missing history.

I later checked whether changing the cutoff would help. The cutoff is the score a text needs before the system flags it. Lowering it catches more generated texts, but can also flag more published ones.

The best cutoff I found after looking at the second test's answers reached about 68% accuracy. It also wrongly flagged more than a third of the published texts. And because I chose it after seeing the answers, that result was not a fresh test on new writing.

That trade-off matters. A higher accuracy number is not automatically a better result for the people whose work receives a warning.

## What I would ask before using a detector

This study tested one basic approach. Other tools may perform differently and need their own checks.

Before using one, I would ask:

- Did the test include writing like the documents I plan to review?
- How many published texts did the tool wrongly flag?
- How much generated writing did it miss?
- Did the people choosing the cutoff already know the test's answers?

I would also look at the writing itself. Are its sources real? Do its claims match those sources? Can the writer explain the work behind it? These questions can lead to evidence that a detector score alone does not provide.

The rules matter too. Editing, translation, and generating a whole draft are different uses of AI. A pattern in sentence lengths does not establish which happened or whether it broke a rule.

If a tool raises a question about someone's work, the people using it should be able to show that it works on that kind of writing. A high percentage from a different test would not be enough for me.
