Four countries or blocs have national AI compute programmes large enough to compare. Each chose a different model. The metric each one publishes to describe its progress says more about the model than the programme's own claims do.

## The US: donations routed to researchers

The United States did not buy a single GPU for its national programme. The National AI Research Resource pilot, launched in January 2024 under the National Science Foundation, coordinates donated compute from private companies and federal agencies and routes researchers and students to the machines.

The NSF NAIRR page, [last updated in March 2026](https://www.nsf.gov/focus-areas/artificial-intelligence/nairr), estimates ~$100 million in private-sector in-kind contributions over the pilot's first two years. The largest contributions: NVIDIA ($30 million in compute and software), Microsoft ($20 million in Azure credits), OpenAI ($1 million in model access), and Cerebras (up to four EXAFLOPs of compute). [28 nongovernmental partners](https://www.nsf.gov/focus-areas/artificial-intelligence/nairr) have contributed.

Fourteen federal agencies participate, from NASA and DARPA to the FDA and the Department of War. The Department of Energy and the National Institutes of Health co-lead a separate secure allocation track called NAIRR Secure for privacy-sensitive AI research.

The NAIRR pilot publishes a running tally on its portal, [nairrpilot.org](https://nairrpilot.org). As of August 2026: **840 research projects, 85 NAIRR Classroom awards, 23 infrastructure and data demonstration projects, 3 community workshops**. The NSF page, last updated March 2026, cited more than 600 projects and 6,000 students across all 50 states, Washington, DC, and Puerto Rico.

The US model does not spend public money on GPUs. It does not build data centres. It coordinates donations and routes researchers to them. Its primary output metric is projects supported. The metric reveals the model: the programme measures throughput, not capacity.

## The UK: one machine bought, one cancelled

The UK's AI Research Resource was [announced in March 2023](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/initial-1-billion-investment-in-uk-s-semiconductor-sector) with £900 million in funding. The first machine, Isambard-AI at the University of Bristol, was built with 5,000 NVIDIA Grace Hopper GH200 superchips at a cost of about £225 million. A second planned site, expected to be located in Cambridge or Edinburgh, did not proceed. [Reports in early 2025](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c5yv0q6mqy8o) said the government scrapped the second machine as part of budget cuts.

The precise final spend is not published in a single document, so the gap between the announced £900 million and the £225 million or so actually spent on the one built machine cannot be closed from published accounts.

The UK now participates in the EU's AI Factory network as an antenna partner, listed as UKAIFA in the [European Commission's AI Factories catalogue](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/ai-factories). A country that cut its national programme joined a European one.

The UK model is straightforward public procurement: buy machines, install them at a university, run them for researchers. Its metric could be machine utilisation. It published the announcement and the machine but not the utilisation.

## The EU: 19 sites, zero published utilisation hours

The EU's AI Factories are the best-documented of the four programmes. The [European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/ai-factories) publishes the full selection history.

| Wave | Date | Sites |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First | December 2024 | 7 consortia, 15 member states |
| Second | March 2025 | 6 more |
| Third | October 2025 | 6 more, plus 13 antennas |

The network now stands at 19 AI Factories and 13 antennas across the EU and partner countries. The Commission says at least nine new AI-optimised supercomputers will be procured, which it says will more than triple the EuroHPC AI computing capacity. Total investment through the EuroHPC Joint Undertaking for the 2021-2027 period is €10 billion, counting EU, member state, and associated country money.

The EU has also opened a call for up to seven AI Gigafactories, facilities of more than 100,000 advanced AI processors each, supported by up to €10 billion in public funding and an estimated €20 billion in private investment.

The EU publishes factory selections, site counts, and partner lists. The [EuroHPC AI Factories page](https://eurohpc-ju.europa.eu/ai-factories_en) says the factories offer "free, customised support" to SMEs and startups. It does not publish running hours delivered to those startups in a comparable format. The factory count is a commitment metric. The hours would be a delivery metric. At 19 factories, the EU has published the most commitments. The delivery ledger is not on the same page.

## India: rented machines, counted but underused

India's programme is covered fully in [the companion piece on this site](/indiaai). The model is empanelment rental: IndiaAI does not buy machines. It empanels private cloud providers, approves users, and subsidises access. MeitY reported 38,000 GPUs onboarded by February 2026. Cash released by that date: ₹400.94 crore, 3.9% of the ₹10,371.92 crore outlay. Press reporting put utilised GPUs at 7,418 of 33,099 committed.

India's primary metric is GPUs onboarded. Its secondary metric, utilisation, is reported by a newspaper.

## What each programme counts

| Programme | Model | Primary metric | Published utilisation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| US NAIRR | Donation + coordination | 840 projects supported | No |
| UK AIRR | Public procurement | 1 machine built (of 2) | No |
| EU AI Factories | Co-federation | 19 sites selected | No |
| India IndiaAI | Empanelment rental | 38,000 GPUs onboarded | No |

None of the four publishes hours of GPU use per unit of public money. The US publishes the metric closest to actual use. The EU publishes the most commitments. The UK cancelled half its programme. India publishes a count that a newspaper has to translate into use.

The differences are not accidents. Each programme's metric reflects what it can publish given its model. The US cannot publish a GPU count because it does not buy GPUs. It can publish projects supported. India can publish an onboarded count because it empanels providers and can count what they report. It has not published utilisation because utilisation comes from a letter to providers, not from the programme's own dashboard.

## What would make the race real

Three things would turn the announcements into a scorecard.

1. **Running hours per unit of public money.** GPU-hours delivered, divided by cash released.
2. **Cost per hour after subsidy.** Published tender prices, as India's PIB did for its L1 bids, with subsidy applied.
3. **Allocation wait times.** Duration between application and access.

The US programme is closest to publishing the first two because it counts projects and its partners' contributions are public. The UK is furthest, because it counted money it did not spend. Until any of the four publishes all three, the national AI compute race is scored on announcements, and the announcement that moves fastest is the next one.
