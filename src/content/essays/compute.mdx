Four countries or blocs have national AI compute programmes large enough to compare. Each chose a different model. The published metrics reflect the model.

## The US

The United States did not buy a single GPU for its national programme. The National AI Research Resource pilot, launched in January 2024 under the National Science Foundation, coordinates donated compute from private companies and federal agencies and routes researchers and students to the machines.

The NSF NAIRR page, [last updated in March 2026](https://www.nsf.gov/focus-areas/artificial-intelligence/nairr), estimates ~$100 million in private-sector in-kind contributions over the pilot's first two years. The largest contributions: NVIDIA ($30 million in compute and software), Microsoft ($20 million in Azure credits), OpenAI ($1 million in model access), and Cerebras (up to four EXAFLOPs of compute). [28 nongovernmental partners](https://www.nsf.gov/focus-areas/artificial-intelligence/nairr) have contributed.

Fourteen federal agencies participate, from NASA and DARPA to the FDA and the Department of War. The Department of Energy and the National Institutes of Health co-lead a separate secure allocation track called NAIRR Secure for privacy-sensitive AI research.

The NAIRR pilot publishes a running tally on its portal, [nairrpilot.org](https://nairrpilot.org). As of August 2026: **840 research projects, 85 NAIRR Classroom awards, 23 infrastructure and data demonstration projects, 3 community workshops**. The NSF page, last updated March 2026, cited more than 600 projects and 6,000 students across all 50 states, Washington, DC, and Puerto Rico.

The US model does not spend public money on GPUs. It does not build data centres. It coordinates donations and routes researchers to them. It measures projects supported.

## The UK

The UK's AI Research Resource was announced in March 2023 with £900 million in funding. The first machine, Isambard-AI at the University of Bristol, was built with 5,000 NVIDIA Grace Hopper GH200 superchips at a cost of about £225 million. A second planned site, expected to be located in Cambridge or Edinburgh, did not proceed. The government was reported in early 2025 to have scrapped the second machine as part of budget cuts.

The precise final spend is not published in a single document, so the gap between the announced £900 million and the £225 million or so actually spent on the one built machine cannot be closed from published accounts.

The UK now participates in the EU's AI Factory network as an antenna partner, listed as UKAIFA in the [European Commission's AI Factories catalogue](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/ai-factories).

The UK model is public procurement. It bought one machine. It did not publish its utilisation.

## The EU

The EU's AI Factories are the best-documented of the four programmes. The [European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/ai-factories) publishes the full selection history.

| Wave | Date | Sites |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First | December 2024 | 7 consortia, 15 member states |
| Second | March 2025 | 6 more |
| Third | October 2025 | 6 more, plus 13 antennas |

The network now stands at 19 AI Factories and 13 antennas across the EU and partner countries. The Commission says at least nine new AI-optimised supercomputers will be procured, which it says will more than triple the EuroHPC AI computing capacity. Total investment through the EuroHPC Joint Undertaking for the 2021-2027 period is €10 billion, counting EU, member state, and associated country money.

The EU has also opened a call for up to seven AI Gigafactories, facilities of more than 100,000 advanced AI processors each, supported by up to €10 billion in public funding and an estimated €20 billion in private investment.

The EU publishes factory selections, site counts, and partner lists. The [EuroHPC AI Factories page](https://eurohpc-ju.europa.eu/ai-factories_en) says the factories offer free, customised support to SMEs and startups. It does not publish running hours delivered to those startups in a comparable format. The factory count measures commitments. Utilisation hours, if published, would measure delivery.

## India

India's programme is covered fully in [the companion piece on this site](/indiaai). The model is empanelment rental: IndiaAI does not buy machines. It empanels private cloud providers, approves users, and subsidises access. MeitY reported 38,000 GPUs onboarded by February 2026. Cash released by that date: ₹400.94 crore, 3.9% of the ₹10,371.92 crore outlay. Press reporting put utilised GPUs at 7,418 of 33,099 committed. Both figures are documented in [the IndiaAI parliamentary record](/indiaai).

India's primary published metric is GPUs onboarded. Utilisation was reported by a newspaper, not by the programme.

## France

France anchored its national AI compute commitment in private investment. At the February 2025 AI Action Summit in Paris, President Macron announced €109 billion in private AI investment commitments. The French government committed €2.5 billion in public funding. The country's compute capacity sits largely within private data centres rather than a dedicated state procurement programme.

The EU AI Factories fill the public-side gap. The first French AI Factory, AI2F, was selected in the second wave in March 2025, tied to the Jean Zay supercomputer at GENCI. A second French factory was announced in the third wave in October 2025. France also hosts a portion of the MGX- and G42-backed data centre plans covered in [the sovereign AI debt piece on this site](/aidebt), with pledged investment of up to $52 billion.

The French model tests whether a country can count private pledges as national capacity. The answer depends on whether the pledges convert to running hours the government can audit.

## Japan

Japan's programme is not a single named mission but a series of subsidies and joint ventures anchored by SoftBank, the parent company of ARM. The government has budgeted over ¥100 billion for AI compute subsidies through 2026, and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry has backed domestic AI infrastructure through state-backed lenders.

SoftBank is the chairman and largest equity holder of Stargate, covered in [the sovereign AI debt piece on this site](/aidebt). SoftBank's announced $19 billion equity commitment is largely funded through bank borrowing from Mizuho and other Japanese lenders. Japan's national AI compute is, in practice, SoftBank's balance sheet.

## What each programme counts

| Programme | Model | Primary metric | Published utilisation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| US NAIRR | Donation + coordination | 840 projects supported | No |
| UK AIRR | Public procurement | 1 machine built (of 2) | No |
| EU AI Factories | Co-federation | 19 sites selected | No |
| India IndiaAI | Empanelment rental | 38,000 GPUs onboarded | No |
| France | Private pledges + EU federation | Private investment counted as capacity | No |
| Japan | Subsidies + SoftBank debt | SoftBank balance sheet | No |

No programme publishes hours of GPU use per unit of public money. The US publishes projects supported. The EU publishes sites selected. India publishes GPUs onboarded. The UK published an announcement and then stopped.

The differences follow from the model. The US does not buy GPUs, so it cannot publish a GPU count. It publishes projects. India empanels providers, so it can count what providers report. It has not published utilisation because utilisation comes from a letter to providers, not from a programme dashboard.

## What would make the race real

Three things would turn the announcements into a scorecard.

1. **Running hours per unit of public money.** GPU-hours delivered, divided by cash released.
2. **Cost per hour after subsidy.** Published tender prices, as India's PIB did for its L1 bids, with subsidy applied.
3. **Allocation wait times.** Duration between application and access.

The US programme publishes the most user-facing data because it counts projects and its partners' contributions are public. The UK published the least, because it counted money it did not spend. None publishes all three metrics.
