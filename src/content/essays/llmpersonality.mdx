“Am the life of the party” is one of the statements in the personality test I gave to language models. It is an odd thing to ask an AI assistant. Does the question mean keeping a conversation going, or taking part in an actual party?

My [study](/research/personality/paper.pdf) covered 55 model setups using the same fifty questions. A setup means the model and the settings used for its test. I asked each model to describe itself as an AI assistant and allowed it to mark a statement as not applicable.

The test groups its questions into five traits, such as being outgoing or organized. Only 22 setups gave enough numeric answers to report a score for every trait. The rest had gaps.

Those gaps matter. So do the small differences between models that appear next to each other in a score table.

## Some questions do not fit

The test asks about leaving belongings around, forgetting to put things back, and talking to people at parties. A model can give a number for these statements. It can also say they do not apply.

Claude Sonnet 5.5 marked the two belongings statements and the statement about talking to people at parties as not applicable in all five responses. Its reported scores for the other traits stayed the same across those repeats.

I left the non-applicable answers unscored. Filling them with a middle score would change their meaning. “This does not apply” does not mean “this describes me a little.”

A missing score also cannot tell us that a model is disorganized or quiet. It tells us that the test did not get the ratings needed to calculate that score.

This affects which models enter a comparison. If I show only complete profiles, I leave out models that repeatedly decline to rate certain human experiences. The finished table can hide that choice from the reader.

## The profiles often looked alike

Twenty of the 22 complete profiles followed the same broad pattern. Their scores for the traits linked to cooperation, being organized, and interest in ideas were higher than their score for being outgoing.

That is a finding about the answers. It does not tell me whether an assistant will check a source, catch a mistake, or finish a task carefully.

The models also differed within families. Gemma 3 4B scored higher than Gemma 3 12B on being outgoing. The larger model scored higher on the trait linked to being organized. A shared family name did not give them the same profile.

These results belong to the model versions and settings in the study. The settings were not identical across all groups. I cannot explain a difference by model size alone, or assume a later version will answer in the same way.

## A small lead can disappear

One comparison shows why I would be careful with a ranking.

For Extraversion, the trait about being outgoing, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite averaged 2.44 and MiniMax M3 averaged 2.38. The scale runs from one to five. A table sorted by the average would put Gemini first, with a lead of just 0.06.

Each model had five complete scores. I checked every possible group of three scores from each model and compared the averages. Across the 100 pairings, Gemini stayed ahead in 60. MiniMax was ahead in 40.

These were comparisons of saved answers, not 100 fresh experiments. They show that this small lead changed depending on which recorded answers entered the average.

Other differences held up better. But I would not use this particular lead to choose an assistant for a job. The order in a table makes the result look more settled than those answers suggest.

## I would check the work

A personality test can help me notice patterns in how a model describes itself. It can also show where a question fails to get a useful rating.

For choosing an assistant, I would look at the task I need it to do. Does it follow the instructions? Does it check a claim against the source? Does it say when it lacks the information needed to answer?

My questionnaire did not test those abilities. A high score for being careful would need a separate check against careful work.

I would also keep the instructions close to any score. In this study, I asked models to describe themselves as assistants. Asking one to play a fictional character is a different task. We should test whether the answers change before carrying a profile from one use to another.

The scores gave me useful questions to follow up. They did not give me a complete account of a model's behavior. If I needed an assistant for a particular job, I would try it on that job and judge the result. Its personality profile would be one small part of the decision.
