The biggest AI compute projects of the past two years are not funded like startups. They are funded like national infrastructure, with sovereign money, convertible instruments, and bank debt. The public discussion treats the announcements as investment. The records show a different chain, from announced, to funded, to spent, and the three numbers rarely match.

The pattern is the same one visible in the Sarvam deal covered in [the IndiaAI piece](/indiaai), where a government compute subsidy was structured as compulsorily convertible debentures that may become an equity stake. The sovereign version runs at a different scale. This piece follows the money on three vehicles, Stargate, MGX, and G42, and then asks who actually bears the risk.

## Stargate: $500 billion announced, borrowed in tranches

Stargate was announced on 21 January 2025 at the White House as a joint venture of OpenAI, SoftBank, Oracle, and MGX, with plans to spend up to $500 billion on US AI infrastructure by 2029. The announced numbers and the funding structure were reported in detail.

The Wall Street Journal reported in April 2025 that SoftBank, the main financial backer, was expected to put in only about 10% equity, with the rest coming from debt or debt-like funding. That detail matters. The first $10 billion of SoftBank's commitment was expected to be borrowed from Mizuho and other Japanese lenders. In May 2025, JPMorgan agreed to lend $2.3 billion for the Abilene, Texas data centre project, which is leased to Oracle.

The gap between announced and spent became visible in August 2025. [Bloomberg reported on 7 August 2025](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-08-07/softbank-concedes-stargate-project-with-openai-needs-more-time) that the project had not started and that no funds had been raised to meet the $500 billion budget. SoftBank conceded it needed more time, citing market uncertainty and AI hardware valuations.

Then the announcements resumed. On 23 September 2025, OpenAI said Stargate would add five new data centre sites, taking planned capacity to nearly 7 gigawatts and claiming over $400 billion in investment over three years. In October 2025, a $25 billion, 500-megawatt project was announced for Patagonia, Argentina, with a partner called Sur Energy. The five-site announcement was about the infrastructure plan. The August Bloomberg report was about the money. Both are true, and they describe different stages of the same project.

## MGX: the fund with a target, and the fund that raised

MGX is the Abu Dhabi state investment vehicle for AI, launched in March 2024 with Mubadala and G42 as founding partners and a reported target of $100 billion in assets under management. Its chair is Sheikh Tahnoon bin Zayed, the UAE's national security adviser.

The vehicles it has touched are a ledger of their own. In September 2024, MGX joined BlackRock and Microsoft in the Global AI Infrastructure Investment Partnership, a fund for data centres and power infrastructure. In January 2025 it took a stake in Stargate. In October 2025, a consortium of MGX, AIP, and BlackRock's Global Infrastructure Partners agreed to buy Aligned Data Centers for $40 billion, reported as the largest data centre deal to date. In June 2026, [Bloomberg reported](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-23/abu-dhabi-s-mgx-raises-about-50-billion-to-accelerate-ai-deals) that MGX had raised close to $50 billion from regional and global investors for an AI infrastructure fund.

The sequence matters. A $100 billion target in 2024, a $40 billion acquisition in 2025, a $50 billion fund raise in 2026. Each step is documented. What is not documented is what portion of the fund is deployed versus reserved, because MGX does not publish a portfolio ledger. The acquisition of Aligned Data Centers is a spent item. The fund raise is a funded item. The gap between them is the part that is not public.

## G42: the company that trades compute access for licences

G42 is the Emirati AI group chaired by the same Sheikh Tahnoon bin Zayed. Its history shows the exchange that defines sovereign AI, capital and compute access against regulatory clearance.

In July 2023, G42 agreed to pay about $100 million for the first of up to nine Cerebras supercomputers. In April 2024, Microsoft announced a $1.5 billion investment in G42, with Microsoft's Brad Smith joining the board and G42 committing to Azure. US lawmakers had asked for an intelligence assessment of G42's ties to China before the deal could advance, and the company said it had divested its China holdings.

The licence ledger followed. In November 2025, the US Department of Commerce authorised the export of advanced semiconductors equivalent to 35,000 NVIDIA Blackwell chips to G42. In July 2026, the US reclassified the UAE to Country Group A:5 under export control rules and designated G42 as an authorised receiving entity, meaning eligible exporters could ship certain advanced AI chips without individual licences. The same company that was under scrutiny for China ties in 2023 became a designated US-licensed recipient in 2026.

The compute announcements scale with the licences. In June 2025, G42 launched its Europe unit with pledges of up to $52 billion for French data centres and $40 billion for Italy. In May 2025, it joined the Stargate UAE project with OpenAI, Oracle, NVIDIA, SoftBank, and Cisco, with initial operations expected in 2026. In February 2026 it signed a framework with a Vietnamese consortium for up to $1 billion in data centre capacity, and announced it would build a national-scale supercomputer for India. In November 2025, Microsoft and G42 announced a 200-megawatt data centre expansion in the UAE.

The French and Italian pledges are the ones to watch. They are announced numbers attached to letters of intent, not spent money. The 35,000-chip licence is a capacity authorisation, not a purchase order. G42 does not publish which came first, the licence, the pledge, or the ground breaking.

## Who bears the risk

The instruments matter because they allocate the downside. Stargate's structure puts the bulk of the money as debt against a small equity base, with SoftBank's reported 10% equity participation and Mizuho and JPMorgan providing loans. If the AI hardware market cools, the lenders hold the exposure and the equity partners hold the project. The government of Japan, through its banks, is effectively financing US AI infrastructure. The government of Abu Dhabi, through MGX, is acquiring data centres outright, which means it owns the assets if the leases hold.

The Sarvam pattern from the IndiaAI piece is the small version of the same trade. A government supplies subsidised compute and receives convertibles that may become 1-2% of the company. MGX and G42 are the national version, where the state supplies capital and licences and receives either equity or infrastructure ownership. The accountability question is the same in both cases. What happens to the public interest if the assets underperform?

## What would prove the structure works

Three documents would close the gap between announced, funded, and spent.

1. **Deployment schedules with dates.** Stargate's five sites have announcement dates. Ground-breaking dates and power-connection dates would show progress.
2. **Equity-to-debt ratios at close.** The WSJ report on SoftBank's 10% equity stake is the most important single number in this piece. Published capital structures would make every project comparable.
3. **Portfolio holdings with valuations.** MGX's $50 billion fund has a target. A published list of deployed capital, at cost, would show the difference between raised and spent.

The announcements will keep coming, because announcements are free and ground breaking is not. The test will be whether the sovereign funds publish what they spent, not what they pledged. Until they do, the AI build-out is a credit market with press releases, and the risk sits with whoever financed the last tranche.
