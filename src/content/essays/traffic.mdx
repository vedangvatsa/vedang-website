In August 2026, 35.6% of traffic on Cloudflare's network came from bots. Most of that is still search and SEO. Ads, subscriptions, and affiliates still need a person on the page. An AI crawler that copies the HTML and refers almost nobody still costs the origin a fetch.

Cloudflare already drops about 6% of global traffic as malicious, and millions of customers have turned on AI crawler blocking, so the raw bot share sits higher than the published number.

Three cloud providers originate nearly a third of all bot traffic. Amazon at 14.7% across two AS numbers. Microsoft at 9%. Google at 8.5%. The United States originates 42.6% of all bot requests.

<img src="/images/essays/traffic-cloud-bots.svg?v=2" alt="Bot traffic origination by cloud provider" width="400" height="280" class="w-full my-8" />

The source is [Cloudflare Radar](https://radar.cloudflare.com/bots): 330 cities, 81 million HTTP requests per second. That is as close as the public internet gets to a full count.

Most of this traffic is not AI. The [Cloudflare 2025 Year in Review](https://blog.cloudflare.com/radar-2025-year-in-review/) found that search engine crawlers made up 40% of verified bot traffic. AI crawlers accounted for 20%. SEO bots, the commercial scraping infrastructure that monitors rankings and backlinks, made up 13%. Googlebot alone generated more than a quarter of all verified bot traffic and 4.5% of HTML requests. All other AI bots combined generated 4.2%.

<img src="/images/essays/traffic-composition.svg?v=3" alt="Verified bot traffic composition by category" width="400" height="300" class="w-full my-8" />

User-action crawling grew 15x in 2025, faster than search, training, or SEO bots. Someone asks a chatbot a question. The chatbot fetches a page. The person reads the answer and never sees the site.

The "more than half the web is bots" line comes from Imperva's [2016 Bot Traffic Report](https://www.imperva.com/resources/resource-library/infographics/bot-traffic-report-2016/): 16.7 billion visits, 100,000 domains, 51.8% automated. The [2023 report](https://www.imperva.com/blog/2023-imperva-bad-bot-report-key-learnings/) put 2022 traffic at 47.4%. The [2024 report](https://www.imperva.com/company/press_releases/bots-make-up-half-of-all-internet-traffic-globally/) put 2023 traffic at 49.6%. [Dead internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Internet_theory) people treated those reports as proof. Everyone else treated them as conspiracy-adjacent. The later measurements did not make the conspiracy true. They made the traffic claim hard to laugh off.

## When a conspiracy becomes a measurement

The dead internet theory originated in a [5 January 2021 post](https://forum.agoraroad.com/index.php?threads/dead-internet-theory-most-of-the-internet-is-fake.3011/) by a user named IlluminatiPirate on the forum Agora Road's Macintosh Cafe. The post said the internet had turned into automated content, steered by algorithms and, in the author's telling, coordinated state actors. The state-actor part made it easy to ignore. The automated-content part did not stay easy to ignore.

Kaitlyn Tiffany brought the theory into mainstream coverage with an [31 August 2021 article in The Atlantic](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2021/08/dead-internet-theory-wrong-but-feels-true/619937/). In 2024, Yoshija Walter argued in [AI and Society](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-023-01857-0) that the "once speculative theory" was becoming observable as AI-generated content proliferated. In 2026, Hal Berghel published in [Computer](https://doi.org/10.1109/MC.2025.3616665) what he called a "leaner" version of the theory, "stripped of paranoia, prejudice, politics and polemic." Berghel argued that dismissing the core observations because conspiracy theorists attach implausible claims to them would be "a mistake of the first order."

Then CEOs started repeating it. In May 2025, [Bloomberg reported](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-05-08/maybe-ai-slop-is-killing-the-internet-after-all) that tools like ChatGPT and Midjourney had made the theory "look downright prophetic." In September 2025, Sam Altman [posted on X](https://x.com/sama/status/1963366714684707120) that he had never taken the theory that seriously, "but it seems like there are really a lot of LLM-run twitter accounts now." [Time](https://time.com/7316046/sam-altman-dead-internet-theory/) covered the post on 10 September.

In October 2025, [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/conormurray/2025/10/13/ohanian-and-altman-warn-of-dead-internet-theory-what-is-it-and-how-is-ai-making-it-happen/) wrote that Reddit co-founder Alexis Ohanian had become vocal on the same point. In June he had [posted](https://x.com/alexisohanian/status/1931712872343847154) that most of what people see online is already AI-generated. Four years from a forum post to people who run platforms saying the traffic claim out loud.

In March 2024, [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/facebook-users-say-amen-bizarre-ai-generated-images-jesus-rcna143965) found Facebook pages posting generated pictures of Jesus built from shrimp, crabs, and seahorses, often with a caption claiming someone had made the work by hand. Commenters typed Amen by the thousand. [404 Media](https://www.404media.co/facebooks-algorithm-is-boosting-ai-spam-that-links-to-ai-generated-ad-laden-click-farms/) followed those pages: many were spam accounts that later pushed people toward ad farms and scams. The 2021 forum post said the internet was turning into automated content. This was that, without a secret agency behind it.

In December 2024, Meta announced AI-powered autonomous accounts on Facebook and Instagram. Connor Hayes, VP of product for generative AI, told the [Financial Times](https://www.ft.com/content/91184c4e-c6e1-4bb9-b7e5-4e4abef1685c) these AI characters "will have bios and profile pictures and be able to generate and share content powered by AI on the platform." The accounts were removed after backlash.

In January 2026, Ohanian and Kevin Rose opened a Digg public beta. Two months later the CEO, Justin Mezzell, [shut the beta](https://www.theverge.com/tech/894803/digg-beta-shutdown-layoffs-ai) and downsized the team. He wrote that they had banned tens of thousands of accounts and still could not keep bots from wrecking the vote. In May 2026 the brand came back as an [AI-first news product](https://startupfortune.com/digg-is-trying-to-make-ai-aggregation-feel-human-again/).

## How crawlers work, and how they lie

Web crawlers have existed since 1994, when Martijn Koster proposed the [Robots Exclusion Protocol](https://www.robotstxt.org/robotstxt.html) after his server was overwhelmed by a poorly written crawler. The protocol has been voluntary for 32 years. A website puts a robots.txt file on its server. A crawler is supposed to read it and obey. Nobody enforces it. [RFC 9309](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9309), published in 2022, states explicitly that robots.txt rules "are not a form of access authorization." It is a request.

Every major AI company publishes documentation for its crawlers. [OpenAI lists 4](https://platform.openai.com/docs/bots): GPTBot for training, OAI-SearchBot for search, ChatGPT-User for user-driven visits, and OAI-AdsBot for ad verification. [Google publishes](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawlers-fetchers/google-common-crawlers) Googlebot for search and AI training combined, plus Google-Extended as an opt-out for Gemini training. [Perplexity lists](https://docs.perplexity.ai/guides/bots) PerplexityBot for search and Perplexity-User for user visits. Anthropic's documentation sits behind a support page.

The docs do not bind the crawlers.

In August 2025, Cloudflare [published](https://blog.cloudflare.com/perplexity-is-using-stealth-undeclared-crawlers-to-evade-website-no-crawl-directives/) that Perplexity was spoofing a Chrome user agent, rotating IPs across AS numbers, and sometimes not fetching robots.txt. Cloudflare put the declared crawler at 20 to 25 million daily requests and the stealth one at another 3 to 6 million, then de-listed Perplexity from its verified bot directory. Perplexity [said](https://www.perplexity.ai/hub/blog/agents-or-bots-making-sense-of-ai-on-the-open-web) Cloudflare had mixed user-driven fetches with scrapers and blamed some of the stealth traffic on Browserbase.

OpenAI's ChatGPT-User fetched robots.txt and followed it. So a crawler can obey. Some choose not to.

Googlebot is the harder problem. [Cloudflare's September 2025 AI bot principles blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/building-a-better-internet-with-responsible-ai-bot-principles/) identified the issue: Googlebot crawls for both search indexing and AI training in a single pass. A website that wants to appear in Google Search must allow Googlebot. That same crawl feeds Google's AI models. Google-Extended, the opt-out for Gemini training, explicitly does not apply to AI Overviews, which are attached to search. As Cloudflare put it, the combined crawler "forces an impossible choice onto website owners."

OpenAI separates its crawlers by purpose. A publisher can allow OAI-SearchBot and block GPTBot. Google offers no equivalent separation.

## The 70,900 to 1 problem

In July 2024, Cloudflare [published](https://blog.cloudflare.com/declaring-your-aindependence-block-ai-bots-scrapers-and-crawlers-with-a-single-click/) its first network-wide survey of AI crawler traffic. The top 4 crawlers by request volume were Bytespider from ByteDance, Amazonbot, ClaudeBot from Anthropic, and GPTBot from OpenAI. Bytespider accessed 40.40% of Cloudflare-protected websites. GPTBot accessed 35.46%. ClaudeBot reached 11.17%. Among the top 1 million internet properties, 38.73% were accessed by AI bots and only 2.98% took action to block them. Among the top 10 sites by traffic, 80% were accessed and 40% blocked.

The 2025 data shifted. Googlebot's crawl volume dwarfed every other AI bot. Anthropic's ClaudeBot crawling doubled in the first half of 2025 before declining. PerplexityBot grew 3.5x. Bytespider continued the decline it started in 2024.

In July 2025 Cloudflare published a better metric than volume: the [crawl-to-refer ratio](https://blog.cloudflare.com/ai-search-crawl-refer-ratio-on-radar/). Pages crawled versus visitors sent back.

Search used to crawl you and send people back. That deal paid for most of the open web. Google crawled at roughly 3 pages per referred visitor in early 2025. DuckDuckGo stayed below 1:1. Bing ranged between 50 and 70:1.

AI platforms were nowhere near those ratios. In the [July 2025 Radar post](https://blog.cloudflare.com/ai-search-crawl-refer-ratio-on-radar/), Cloudflare put Anthropic as high as 70,900 HTML pages crawled per referred visitor. By [July 2025 in a later write-up](https://blog.cloudflare.com/crawlers-click-ai-bots-training/), the same firm's table had Anthropic at 286,930 to 1 in January and 38,066 to 1 in July. OpenAI peaked at 3,700 to 1 in March 2025, then fell as ChatGPT search gained usage. Perplexity stayed the most referral-friendly of the big AI names, below 200 to 1 from September 2025 onward. The 70,900 figure is a snapshot, not a constant. Even the improved July number is still four orders of magnitude worse than Google.

[Cloudflare's CEO wrote](https://blog.cloudflare.com/content-independence-day-no-ai-crawl-without-compensation/) that getting traffic from OpenAI is 750 times harder than it was from Google in the previous decade. Getting traffic from Anthropic is 30,000 times harder. On 1 July 2025, Cloudflare declared "Content Independence Day" and announced it would default to blocking AI crawlers that do not pay creators.

## What publishers see when the traffic stops

[Pew Research Center published data in July 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/) from 68,879 Google searches across 900 US adults. Users who saw an AI-generated summary clicked on a traditional search result 8% of the time. Users who did not see a summary clicked 15% of the time. Users clicked on a link within the AI summary itself in 1% of visits.

The same study found that 18% of Google searches produced an AI summary in March 2025. Wikipedia, YouTube, and Reddit supplied 15% of the sources inside those summaries. Government websites appeared more often in AI summaries than in standard results.

[SparkToro's 2024 study](https://sparktoro.com/blog/2024-zero-click-search-study/) put US Google searches that ended without a click at 58.5%. Mobile was worse than desktop then, and worse again later. AI Overviews launched in [May 2024](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/generative-ai-google-search-may-2024/). Google was already answering a majority of queries on the results page. The summaries made that cheaper for Google and thinner for publishers.

In March 2024, Google acknowledged its search results were being flooded by websites that "feel like they were created for search engines instead of people." The company's [blog post](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/google-search-update-march-2024/) on its March 2024 core update promised to reduce "low-quality, unoriginal content" in search results by up to 40%. The AI that was scraping the web was also filling it with content designed to be scraped.

## The protocols try to catch up

The IETF formalized robots.txt as [RFC 9309](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9309) in September 2022. The standard explicitly states it is "not a form of access authorization," only a request. In April 2026, the IETF AI Preferences Working Group published [draft-ietf-aipref-vocab-06](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-aipref-vocab/), defining 2 categories: Train-AI for model training and Search for indexed content. The draft is a first try at machine-readable flags that say "train" and "search" separately.

Cloudflare shipped faster than the IETF. It launched 1-click AI crawler blocking in July 2024, [AI Crawl Control analytics](https://blog.cloudflare.com/cloudflare-ai-audit-control-ai-content-crawlers/) in September 2024, and a Pay Per Crawl marketplace in July 2025. The marketplace lets publishers set a price per 1,000 tokens for AI crawler access, authenticated through Cloudflare's verified bot framework.

[The Internet Archive](https://archive.org/about/) has crawled and preserved the web since 1996. Its crawler obeys robots.txt and does not train AI models. It still consumes the same server resources as AI training crawlers.

## The browser that is also an agent

Browser agents will make text crawlers look cheap. Anthropic announced [Claude computer use](https://docs.anthropic.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/computer-use) in October 2024, enabling Claude to view screens, move cursors, click buttons, and type. Google launched [Project Mariner](https://deepmind.google/technologies/project-mariner/) in December 2024, a Chrome extension that controls the browser to complete tasks. The open-source [Browser Use](https://github.com/browser-use/browser-use) framework lets any AI agent navigate websites programmatically.

A text crawl fetches 2 or 3 HTTP requests. A browser agent loads every image, every JavaScript bundle, every tracking pixel, every analytics call. It renders the page, screenshots it, and feeds the image to a vision model. A single agent browsing session generates hundreds of requests across CDNs, analytics services, and ad networks. The resource consumption of 1 agent browser visit may equal 100 traditional crawler requests.

A crawler at least indexes you. A browser agent uses the page to book a flight, file a ticket, or check a price, then leaves. Nobody saw your brand. Nobody subscribed. Nobody hit an ad. You still paid to serve the page. The money landed somewhere else.

The agents are browsing sites right now.

## Who pays for the web when machines do the reading

A website that serves a billion pages to humans generates revenue from the humans who arrive. A website that serves a billion pages to AI crawlers generates infrastructure costs.

The Cloudflare Pay Per Crawl marketplace is the first attempt at a settlement layer. It charges per content type: news, research, reference. It uses verified bot identity to authenticate who is crawling. Whether it works depends on whether AI companies find paying cheaper than the legal risk of crawling without payment.

[The New York Times sued OpenAI and Microsoft in December 2023](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/12/27/business/media/new-york-times-open-ai-microsoft-lawsuit.html) over training-data ingestion. On 30 April 2024, eight Alden papers including the Chicago Tribune and the Denver Post [filed their own suit](https://www.reuters.com/legal/us-newspapers-sue-openai-copyright-infringement-over-ai-training-2024-04-30/) in the same district. Those cases are about whether copyright covers the crawl. They have not produced a holding yet.

Google's [AI Overviews](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/generative-ai-google-search-may-2024/) launched to US users in May 2024. Google says the boxes include source links. [Pew's July 2025 numbers](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/) are the click count: 8% versus 15% when the box is absent, and 1% on a link inside the box.

## The open data paradox

[Common Crawl](https://commoncrawl.org/faq) is a non-profit that maintains a free, open repository of web crawl data used extensively for AI training. Its crawler, CCBot, obeys robots.txt and supports crawl-delay. The foundation published [dedicated IP ranges](https://commoncrawl.org/ccbot) for verification in November 2024. Its [July 2026 archive](https://commoncrawl.org/blog/july-2026-crawl-archive-now-available) contains 2.14 billion web pages, 364 TiB of uncompressed content. Its [web graph for May to July 2026](https://commoncrawl.org/blog/host--and-domain-level-web-graphs-may-june-and-july-2026) spans 240.4 million nodes and 3.7 billion edges. The foundation also published an [AI Visibility Audit](https://commoncrawl.org/blog/introducing-the-ai-visibility-audit) for publishers.

Common Crawl publishes the crawl and obeys robots.txt. Commercial labs then train on that dump and still send almost no traffic back. The archive did not make that ratio. The labs did.

## Bots have always been here

The web has had robots since 1994. The [robotstxt.org](https://www.robotstxt.org/) resource defines them as "programs that traverse the Web automatically." In 2017, [The Atlantic published](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2017/01/bots-bots-bots/515043/) "The Internet Is Mostly Bots." In 2018, [New York Magazine ran](https://nymag.com/intelligencer/2018/12/how-much-of-the-internet-is-fake.html) "How Much of the Internet Is Fake? Turns Out, a Lot of It, Actually," cataloguing fake YouTube views, fake social media followers, and programmatic ad fraud.

The difference between 2018 and 2026 is what the bots are doing. The 2018 bots sold fake engagement: fake views, fake likes, stolen ad impressions. A fake view did not make a real view worth less. The 2026 bots read the actual page so a model can answer without sending the click. That does make the real visit worth less.

## Who meters the machines

Ads and subscriptions only work if a person shows up. A crawler does not click, subscribe, watch an ad, or come back. Crawl traffic used to be cheap enough to ignore. A 70,900-to-1 ratio is not. That site is paying Anthropic's bandwidth out of its own hosting invoice.

Licensing will cover the Times and a handful of other names. The lawsuit will settle. Forums, blogs, and local papers, which supplied a lot of the training text, will not get a deal. They have no lawyers and no bargaining power.

Metering is Cloudflare's Pay Per Crawl: a private price list on a private network. Robots.txt was a protocol anyone could implement. Pay Per Crawl only works if you sit on Cloudflare. The IETF draft is years out. Anyone who ignored robots.txt can ignore that too.

If crawl traffic is free to take and expensive to serve, sites lock it down. Paywalls, logins, bot challenges, apps. The companies that wanted an open crawl are the reason more of the web will sit behind a wall. The sites that stayed open the longest pay the most for staying open.

Publishers are already paying the hosting bill for someone else's training run. The clicks that used to cover that bill are not coming back. Lawsuits, drafts, and marketplaces have not changed the invoice.
